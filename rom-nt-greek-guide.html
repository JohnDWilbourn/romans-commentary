I.Verbal Aspect — the Aorist Tense

Ingressive Aorist

Romans 1:27 — "and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women." The aorist active participle of ἀφίημι ("gave up, abandoned") is ingressive, marking entrance into a state — homosexuality is entered into, not born into, and since entered volitionally it can be exited volitionally.

Constative Aorist

Gathers an entire action — whether momentary or extended — into a single, undivided whole, viewed from the outside as one entirety.

Romans 1:3 — "concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh." ἐγένετο ("was born," from γίνομαι) is a constative aorist: it gathers the entire incarnation process — conception, gestation, birth, the giving of the spark of life by the Father — into one completed historical event. Active voice: Christ himself produces the action of becoming.

Romans 1:28 — "since they did not see fit to acknowledge God." ἐδοκίμασαν (negated, "did not see fit," from δοκιμάζω, "to test and approve") is a constative aorist: they rejected God after careful examination — the same root produces ἀδόκιμος ("disqualified") later in the verse, forming a deliberate paranomasia: you tested God and said no; God tests you and gives you a disqualified mind.

Romans 3:3 — "What if some were unfaithful?" ἠπίστησαν ("were unfaithful," from ἀπιστέω) is a constative aorist gathering all Jewish unbelief into a single entirety — an informed, deliberate rejection of the gospel, not mere ignorance.

Romans 3:23 — "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." ἥμαρτον ("have sinned," from ἁμαρτάνω) is a constative/gnomic aorist gathering every sin of the entire human race, from Adam's fall to the end of history, into a single axiomatic statement admitting no exception but Christ.

Romans 3:15 — "Their feet are swift to shed blood." The aorist active infinitive of ἐκχέω ("to pour out," with αἷμα, "blood") forms the standing idiom for murder — a momentary act, drawn from Isaiah 59:7.

Romans 1:4 — "and was declared to be the Son of God in power... by his resurrection from the dead." ὁρισθέντος ("having been declared/marked out," from ὁρίζω) is a constative aorist passive participle gathering the entire first-advent package — incarnation, cross, resurrection, ascension, session — into a single decreed whole made in eternity past. (Also treatable under Voice: the passive means Christ received the appointment rather than claiming it.)

Romans 4:2 — "For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God." ἐδικαιώθη ("was justified") gathers the hypothetical act of being justified by works into one entirety, implying an entire system of self-righteousness rather than a single deed.

Romans 4:3 — "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness." Ἐπίστευσεν ("believed") gathers Abraham's act of believing into a single point — instantaneous, non-meritorious faith.

Romans 4:17 — "before the God in whom he believed." ἐπίστευσεν here gathers into one entirety every instance of Abraham's faith-rest across his whole life, not one isolated act.

Romans 4:19 — "he considered his own body, which was as good as dead." κατενόησεν ("considered") gathers Abraham's thirteen-year comprehension of his own condition into a single point of total cognizance.

Romans 4:20 — "No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God." διεκρίθη ("waver") gathers the entire thirteen-year period of testing into one point of evaluation: he did not waver, not once, across the whole span.

Romans 4:25 — "who was delivered up for our trespasses." παρεδόθη ("was delivered up") gathers the entire ~three-hour judicial event of the cross into one completed whole.

Romans 5:1 — "since we have been justified by faith." δικαιωθέντες ("having been justified") gathers the whole of salvation adjustment — imputation followed immediately by justification — into one momentary action.

Romans 5:6 — "Christ died for the ungodly." ἀπέθανεν ("died") gathers the approximately three hours of the cross, during which all human sin was imputed to Christ and judged, into a single historical event.

Romans 5:8 — "Christ died for us." The same verb, ἀπέθανεν, again constative — the whole substitutionary event as one completed act.

Romans 5:9 — "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood." δικαιωθέντες here is again constative, gathering the entirety of salvation adjustment into a single completed action.

Romans 5:12 — "so death spread to all men because all sinned." ἥμαρτον ("sinned") gathers the whole human race into Adam's one act: when Adam sinned, the entire race sinned — not personally, but seminally and federally, as all were present in Adam as the federal head.

Romans 5:14 — "even over those whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam." ἁμαρτήσαντας ("had sinned," a participle) is constative: those living between Adam and Moses did commit personal sins, but those sins are not the issue in view — the reign of death did not depend on them.

Romans 5:16 — "the judgment following one trespass." ἁμαρτήσαντος (a participle behind "trespass") gathers Adam's single act of disobedience into one moment.

Romans 6:2 — "we who died to sin." ἀπεθάνομεν ("died") is a constative aorist referring to a momentary action completed at salvation: the baptism of the Holy Spirit and its result, retroactive positional truth.

Romans 6:3 — "all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death." ἐβαπτίσθημεν ("were baptized") is constative, portraying the whole event as a momentary whole — the instant of salvation adjustment.

Romans 6:4 — "we were buried therefore with him by baptism into death." συνετάφημεν ("were buried with") is constative, referring to a momentary action at salvation, not a process.

Romans 6:6 — "our old self was crucified with him." συνεσταυρώθη ("was crucified with") is constantive, referring to a single completed action at salvation — the co-crucifixion of the old sin nature with Christ.

Romans 6:9 — "Christ, being raised from the dead." ἐγερθείς is constative, gathering the resurrection event as a whole into a single past fact.

Romans 6:10 — "the death he died he died to sin, once for all." The first of two aorist active indicatives of ἀποθνῄσκω here is constative, gathering the entire judicial imputation and judgment of all personal sins into a single whole — the approximately three hours on the cross, viewed in its entirety.

Romans 6:13 (positive command) — "present yourselves to God." The aorist active imperative of παρίστημι spans the entire Christian life as a constative aorist: the action encompasses the period from salvation to physical death or resurrection, with no season during which the command is suspended.

Romans 6:19 — "just as you presented your members as slaves to impurity." παρεστήσατε gathers the entire pre-salvation period — from physical birth to the new birth — into a single unified reality.

Romans 7:2 (protasis) — "if her husband dies she is released from the law." The aorist active subjunctive of ἀποθνῄσκω is constative: it gathers three distinct phases of retroactive positional truth — rejection, separation, and divorce from the first husband — into one unified action.

Romans 7:4 — "you also have died to the law through the body of Christ." Same constative use of ἀποθνῄσκω, gathering identification with Christ in his death, burial, and resurrection into a single completed event.

Romans 7:6 — "we are released from the law, having died to that which held us captive." The aorist participle of ἀποθνῄσκω is constative, viewing the entirety of the baptism of the Holy Spirit — retroactive and current positional truth together — as a single completed event.

Romans 7:7 — "I would not have known sin." The aorist of γινώσκω, negated, gathers Paul's entire prior period of ignorance of the sin nature into a single statement.

Romans 7:11 — "sin... deceived me and through it killed me." The main verbs are constative, gathering the sin nature's entire pattern of deception and killing into a single unified statement of reality.

Romans 8:2 — "the law of the Spirit of life has set you free in Christ Jesus." ἠλευθέρωσεν ("has set free") is constative, gathering the entire action of liberation — the baptism of the Spirit, regeneration, and positional union with Christ — into one unified whole at the instant of salvation.

Romans 8:3 — "he condemned sin in the flesh." κατέκρινεν ("judged/condemned") is constative, gathering the entirety of the judicial imputation of all personal sins in human history to Christ into a single act at the cross — the approximately three hours of his spiritual death.

Romans 8:15 — "you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear." ἐλάβετε (negated) gathers into one entirety the negative reality: the believer has not, taken as a whole, received this thing.

Romans 8:29 — "conformed to the image of his Son" (the larger sentence continues with the golden chain). προέγνω ("foreknew") is a constative aorist, gathering into one entirety the eternal foreknowledge of God — not a process but a single, timeless divine act viewed as a whole.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he justified." ἐδικαίωσεν is constative, gathering the momentary act of imputing divine righteousness at the moment of faith into a single entirety — the second judicial imputation.

Romans 8:32 — "he did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all." παρέδωκεν ("gave him up," from παραδίδωμι) is constative, contemplating the judicial imputation of all personal sins in its entirety — every sin in human history imputed to Christ and judged at one time on the cross.

Culminating (Culminative) Aorist

Emphasis falls on the result of the action rather than the action itself; the transaction is gathered into one whole with stress on what flows from it — permanent, existing consequences.

Romans 1:21 — "For although they knew God, they did not honor him as God." ἐγνόντες (from γινώσκω, "knew") is a culminative aorist: it emphasizes the result of accumulated gospel-hearing — the unbeliever came to full cognizance of the issue of Christ, and that cognizance is the basis of his accountability.

Romans 4:3 — "it was counted to him as righteousness." ἐλογίσθη ("was counted/credited") is a culminative aorist: the crediting of righteousness to Abraham's account, a completed transaction with permanent, existing results. Contrast this with the constative aorist of "believed" in the same verse: the act of faith (constative) versus the permanent result of that act (culminative).

Romans 4:9 — "we say that faith was counted to Abraham as righteousness." ἐλογίσθη again, culminative: the same permanent-result force.

Romans 4:10 — "How then was it counted to him?" ἐλογίσθη, culminative — the crediting itself, viewed from its result.

Romans 4:18 — "In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations." ἐπίστευσεν here is also called consummative: not a single momentary act but Abraham's entire trajectory of faith through decades of spiritual growth, presented as an existing result.

Romans 4:22 — "That is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness." ἐλογίσθη, culminative, closing the inclusio begun at verse 3 with the identical verb and result.

Romans 5:10 — "we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son." κατηλλάγημεν ("were reconciled") views the entire event of salvation adjustment in its completeness, with emphasis on the existing result: reconciliation, received by the believer at the moment of faith, not produced by him.

Romans 5:20 — "Now the law came in to increase the trespass, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more." ἐπλεόνασεν ("increased") and ὑπερεπερίσσευσεν ("abounded all the more") are both culminating aorists in sequence: the first views the imputation of Adam's sin in its entirety with the existing result being the multiplication of personal sins across history; the second views the superabundant supply of grace in its entirety with the existing result being that all those sins have been imputed to Christ at the cross.

Romans 5:21 — "so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life." ἐβασίλευσεν ("reigned") is culminative: it views the imputation of Adam's sin to its genetic home at birth in its entirety, then shifts to the existing result — the sovereignty of the sin nature over human life from birth onward.

Romans 6:6 — "so that the body of sin might be brought to nothing." καταργηθῇ ("might be rendered inoperative," a subjunctive) is a culminative aorist, viewing retroactive positional truth in its entirety but emphasizing the existing result: the ruling power of the sin nature has been broken.

Romans 6:10 — "he died to sin, once for all" (second half of the verse's argument). The second aorist of ἀποθνῄσκω in this verse is culminative, viewing the same judicial imputation with emphasis on the existing result: eternal salvation now available to anyone who believes.

Romans 6:18 — "you became slaves of righteousness." ἐδουλώθητε ("you became enslaved") is a culminative aorist viewing the result of the entire process: the mature believer, having exploited imputed righteousness through a system of doctrine, stands as a slave to the righteousness of God.

Romans 7:9 — "when the commandment came, sin came alive and I died." ἀπέθανον ("I died") is culminative — it does not describe a new event of death but the realization of a condition already existing from birth: spiritual death under the imputation of Adam's original sin. Paul was spiritually dead all along; the tenth commandment brought him to awareness of that condition.

Romans 8:4 — "the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us." πληρωθῇ is a culminative aorist (subjunctive): it gathers into one whole every rebound, every filling of the Spirit, every day of doctrinal perception required to crack the maturity barrier, while emphasizing the result — maturity adjustment and the consequent real imputation of blessing.

Romans 8:29 — "those whom he foreknew he also predestined." προώρισεν ("predestined") is culminative, viewing the decrees of God for the church-age believer in their entirety while regarding the result — the last three steps of the five steps to glory.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he called." ἐκάλεσεν, used in its technical theological sense for election, can also be described as culminative in that it regards the doctrine of election in its entirety from the standpoint of existing result: every Church Age believer is, in fact, elect.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he justified he also glorified." ἐδόξασεν is constative-culminative, gathering into one entirety both the real imputation of blessing in time at maturity and the real imputation of eternal blessing at the judgment seat of Christ.

Romans 8:32 — "he did not spare his own Son." οὐκ ἐφείσατο (see also Dramatic Aorist below) also functions culminatively, viewing the judicial imputation of all personal sins in its entirety and emphasizing the existing results — the entire complex of Church Age blessing that flows from that completed act.

Combinative Aorist

Views an entire, ongoing function as a whole while placing emphasis on the existing result flowing from it — distinguished from the culminative aorist by describing a sustained process (rather than a single transaction) brought to its result.

Romans 4:20 — "he grew strong in his faith." ἐνεδυναμώθη ("grew strong / was strengthened") views the entire, years-long process of Abraham's doctrinal growth as a whole, with emphasis on the existing result: dynamic capacity.

Romans 4:20 — "as he gave glory to God." δοὺς ("having given," an aorist participle) gathers everything that produced Abraham's mature status and presents the result: giving glory to God.

Romans 5:15 — "much more have the grace of God and the free gift... abounded for many." ἐπερίσσευσεν ("abounded," transitive use) is a combinative aorist: it encompasses the entire work of Christ on the cross as a whole while regarding it from the standpoint of its existing results — potential, capacity, and reality all at once.

Dramatic Aorist

States the present reality of a past event with the rhetorical vividness and certitude the dramatic perfect ordinarily supplies — the aorist counterpart to the dramatic perfect, used to make an already-decided doctrinal reality feel immediate and settled.

Romans 7:11 — "sin... deceived me." ἠπάτησεν is a dramatic aorist, emphasizing the present reality of the sin nature's aggression: it does not passively receive the commandment but actively seizes it as a base of operations.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he called" (twice in this verse, and echoed at 8:28's "called according to his purpose"). ἐκάλεσεν (aorist of καλέω, in its technical theological sense for election) is a dramatic aorist: it states the present reality of election with the certitude of a past event fixed in the divine decrees.

Romans 8:32 — "he did not spare his own Son." οὐκ ἐφείσατο is a dramatic aorist bringing the judicial imputation of sin at the cross into sharp, vivid focus.

Gnomic Aorist

States an axiomatic, timeless truth, without reference to a specific past occurrence.

Romans 1:21 — "they became futile in their thinking." ἐματαιώθησαν ("were made futile," passive) is a gnomic aorist passive, stating an axiomatic fact as an invariable present reality: worthless and evil thoughts enter the vacuum left by rejected truth.

Romans 4:21 — "fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised." ποιῆσαι ("to do/accomplish," an infinitive) is gnomic: an axiomatic truth about God's ability in general, not a reference to one past act.

Romans 8:37 — "through him who loved us." The articular aorist active participle of ἀγαπάω here is a gnomic aorist: a fact so fixed in its certainty as to be axiomatic — by Greek idiom this is properly rendered in the English present tense, "who loves us," not "who loved us" as a single past act. God's love for his own righteousness, which every believer possesses through imputation, is timeless, not a completed historical event.

The Aggressive Negative with the Aorist Participle

μή with the aorist participle contemplates the action at its very point of beginning; the negative denies that it even started — stronger than a negation of an ongoing or completed process.

Romans 4:19 — "He did not weaken in faith." μὴ ἀσθενήσας ("not having weakened") is not merely "he did not become weak" over time, but "he did not begin to falter" — there was no onset of doubt at all.

II.Verbal Aspect — the Perfect Tense

Intensive Perfect

Emphasizes the present, standing reality of a completed past action — the strongest available affirmation in Greek that a given state of affairs obtains.

Romans 2:24 — "as it is written." γέγραπται ("it stands written," from γράφω) is a dramatic/intensive perfect passive indicative: a completed action whose results continue permanently — the Old Testament citation (Isaiah 52:5, LXX) carries ongoing, standing force as an expression of divine integrity.

Romans 3:13 — "their throat is an open grave." The perfect passive participle of ἀνοίγω ("opened") is an intensive perfect (perfect of existing state): the tomb has been opened and remains open, with everything that implies about the condition within.

Romans 4:1 — "What then shall we say was gained by Abraham... has found?" εὑρηκέναι ("has found," a perfect infinitive) carries abiding, present force for the argument — not merely "what did Abraham find" in the past, but what he stands as having found, with continuing relevance now.

Romans 4:14 — "faith is null and void, and the promise is void." κεκένωται ("is null and void") is an intensive perfect: faith would stand voided with the result that it is permanently invalidated — the strongest possible affirmation of a settled state.

Romans 5:2 — "we have obtained access." ἐσχήκαμεν ("we have obtained," perfect active of ἔχω) emphasizes a completed action whose results continue permanently: salvation adjustment is an accomplished fact, and as a result the believer now has — and always will have — access into the presence of God.

Romans 5:2 — "this grace in which we stand." ἑστήκαμεν ("we stand," perfect active of ἵστημι) is a dramatic use of the intensive perfect: a vivid assertion that the believer stood in the past and keeps on standing forever — the grammatical anchor of eternal security in this verse.

Romans 6:5 — "if we have been united with him in a death like his." γεγόναμεν (intensive perfect of γίνομαι) directs attention to the existing results of a completed action: the believer has become intimately united to the likeness of Christ's death, and that union permanently stands. The action is complete; the results are present and abiding.

Dramatic Perfect

Emphasizes the existing state resulting from a completed action, stated with rhetorical vividness — used especially for citation formulas and declarations of fact.

Romans 1:1 — "set apart for the gospel of God." ἀφωρισμένος ("having been set apart," from ἀφορίζω, a compound of ἀπό + ὁρίζω) is a dramatic perfect passive participle: the completed, irreversible act of divine appointment on the Damascus road, carried forward as a present, ongoing reality. Passive voice: Paul receives the appointment; God executes it.

Romans 4:17 — "As it is written." γέγραπται is better rendered "as it stands written," not merely "it was written": the canonical text stands in a permanent, present state of authority.

Romans 4:18 — "as he had been told." εἰρημένον ("that which had been spoken") stresses the promise's still-valid, undiminished force.

Romans 4:19 — "his own body, which was as good as dead." νενεκρωμένον ("as good as dead," literally "having been deadened") describes Abraham's body vividly and realistically, with the state persisting to the narrative present.

Romans 4:25 (and Romans 5:8, and John 19:30 as the source citation) — "It is finished." τετέλεσται is the rhetorical use of the intensive perfect, emphasizing that the saving work was completed in its entirety while Christ was still physically alive.

Romans 5:5 — "God's love has been poured into our hearts." ἐκκέχυται ("has been poured out," perfect passive of ἐκχέω) is a dramatic perfect: the action is completed, and the results are permanent and ongoing, saturating the right lobe of the soul through the daily function of doctrine intake.

Romans 6:7 — "one who has died has been set free from sin" (ESV) / "has been acquitted." δεδικαίωται (perfect passive of δικαιόω) is the Greek idiom for presenting the permanent, unalterable character of positional truth: no post-salvation sin, human good, or involvement in evil can revoke this judicial verdict of acquittal from the power of the sin nature.

Romans 7:2 — "a married woman is bound by law to her husband." δέδεται (perfect passive of δέω, "to bind") is a dramatic perfect: from the moment the woman marries, the binding is in effect not provisionally but as a fixed, continuing reality — strong rhetorical emphasis on permanence.

Perfect-as-Present

A perfect tense functioning grammatically like a present, describing a state of knowledge or condition established in the past that is currently, operatively true — distinct from the dramatic perfect in that the emphasis falls on ongoing present function rather than rhetorical vividness about a completed act.

Romans 2:2 — "We know that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things." οἴδαμεν ("we know," from οἶδα) is morphologically a perfect functioning as a present in Koine Greek — a perfective present indicative: the mature believer has arrived at this knowledge through sustained doctrine intake, and it abides as a present operational reality.

Romans 3:19 — "Now we know that whatever the law says it speaks to those who are under the law." οἴδαμεν again functions as a perfective present (see Romans 2:2 above), the settled, abiding knowledge of the mature believer.

Romans 6:9 — "We know that Christ, being raised from the dead, will never die again." εἰδότες (perfect active participle of οἶδα, "to know") functions as a present: a state of knowledge already established and currently operative. The tense assumes inculcation — the apostle writes to believers who have already learned this doctrine, and assumes that learning has produced a current, operative state of cognizance.

Romans 7:18 — "For I know that nothing good dwells in me." οἶδα here is likewise perfect-as-present, and combined with the inferential particle γάρ signals that Paul's self-knowledge is settled, complete, and objective — the capacity for such objectivity is itself a product of doctrinal advance.

Romans 8:26 — "we do not know what to pray for as we ought." οἴδαμεν (negated) is perfect-as-present: the perfective present force here denotes the continuation of ignorance regarding proper prayer specifically in the context of undeserved suffering — not a moral failure, but a condition inherent to the situation itself.

Romans 8:16 — "the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit." The historical present of συμμαρτυρεῖ carries a comparable perfective force to the perfect-as-present verbs above: continuation of existing results, the Spirit's testimony ongoing rather than a single past event, even though it is formally a present rather than a true perfect.

The Perfect of Settled Confidence

A perfect tense (of a verb of persuasion or conviction) denoting a state of confidence established in the past and continuing, unshaken, into the present — distinguished from ordinary perfect-as-present usage by the specifically volitional/emotional content of the settled state.

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind." πέποιθας ("you are confident/persuaded," from πείθω) is perfective-present in force: a settled confidence whose results persist into the present — the self-righteous legalist has established, and persists in, a false confidence about his standing.

Romans 8:38 — "For I am sure that neither death nor life..." πέπεισμαι (perfect passive of πείθω, "to persuade") carries the connotation of settled, unshakeable confidence — a confidence entirely divorced from self and circumstance, grounded instead in the plan and provision of God through doctrine resident in the soul, not a fluctuating feeling.

The Pluperfect Functioning as an Imperfect of Duration

Because οἶδα is already a perfect tense used with present force, its pluperfect form does not carry the usual "had done" sense; instead it functions as an imperfect of duration, describing a state of ignorance persisting up to a specific past point.

Romans 7:7 — "I would not have known what it is to covet." ᾔδειν (pluperfect of οἶδα) functions as an imperfect of duration: Paul's ignorance of the lust pattern persisted up to the specific moment he encountered and processed the tenth commandment — not a single past failure to know, but an extended state of not-knowing.

Consummated Perfect

Places equal weight on the process leading up to completion and on the existing result — distinguished from the intensive perfect (which stresses the settled state alone) and from the culminative aorist (which stresses the result of a single transaction).

Romans 4:14 — "the promise is void." κατήργηται ("is void") places equal stress on the process of cancellation and its ongoing consequence.

Romans 4:17 — "I have made you the father of many nations." τέθεικα ("I have made/appointed," quoting Genesis 17:5) has God speaking as though every son, every nation, every generation of Abraham's progeny is already complete — reviewing the entire future process and presenting it as accomplished, spoken at a moment of total biological impossibility.

Iterative Perfect

Denotes an action or state recurring at intervals, rather than continuously.

Romans 4:21 — "what he had promised." ἐπήγγελται ("he had promised") indicates the promise was made at intervals, not constantly repeated: a mature believer does not require continuous reassurance, and the grammar itself reflects that.

III.Verbal Aspect — the Present Tense

Static Present

Represents a condition as a fixed, unqualified, timeless reality.

Romans 1:9 — "For God is my witness." A static present of εἰμί-type force underlies Paul's appeal — a present tense denoting an unqualified, timeless fact rather than a momentary state.

Romans 1:16 — "for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes." ἔστιν here is a static present of εἰμί: the gospel dogmatically and perpetually is the ability (δύναμις) of God, not merely an occasional demonstration of it.

Romans 1:18 — "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness." The same verb ἀποκαλύπτεται recurs, now as a static present: the just anger of God perpetually exists as the standing posture of divine justice toward the maladjusted — without variation, without exception.

Romans 4:4 — "his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due." οὐ λογίζεται ("is not counted") states the wage principle as a flat, timeless fact.

Romans 4:5 — "his faith is counted as righteousness." λογίζεται ("is counted") asserts the crediting of righteousness as dogmatic reality.

Romans 4:5 — "believes in him who justifies the ungodly." δικαιοῦντα ("justifies," a participle) presents this as a perpetually existing divine activity/character, not a one-time event.

Romans 4:16 — "that the promise may rest [be secure]." εἶναι in this construction presents the promise's security as a perpetually existing condition established at the moment of faith.

Romans 4:21 — "that God was able." δυνατός ἐστιν ("he is able") states God's ability as a permanently existing condition.

Romans 5:5 — "hope does not put us to shame." οὐ καταισχύνει ("does not disappoint") is a static present: a condition perpetually existing, taken as a dogmatic, axiomatic fact — not a conditional promise but an absolute statement of what divine integrity is by nature.

Romans 6:9 — "death no longer has dominion over him." οὐκέτι κυριεύει ("no longer is master") is a static present with the negative temporal adverb οὐκέτι: a condition that perpetually exists — physical death is no longer able to touch the resurrected Christ.

Romans 6:10 — "the life he lives he lives to God." ζῇ (see the Cognate Accusative entry, section XV) denotes a condition that perpetually exists: Christ's resurrection body is permanently beyond corruption, decay, or death.

Romans 6:11 — "consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God." εἶναι (infinitive complement of λογίζομαι) is a static present describing a perpetually existing condition established at a definite past point (salvation) and continuing without interruption.

Romans 7:1 — "the law is binding on a person only as long as he lives." κυριεύει ("is binding," literally "lords it over") is a static present: a self-evident, timeless legal principle, true of the Mosaic law's authority in general, not one instance of it.

Romans 7:2 — "while he is living." ζῶντος (a participle) is a static present expressing a principle held as a permanent, self-evident fact: the binding of the wife endures as long as the husband lives — a general truth, not a report about one couple.

Romans 7:14 — "we know that the law is spiritual." ἐστίν ("is") is static: a timeless characterization of the law's nature.

Romans 8:9 — "assuming that the Spirit of God dwells in you." οἰκεῖ (present active of οἰκέω) is a static present: a condition that perpetually exists, God the Holy Spirit himself producing the action of indwelling every believer without exception.

Romans 8:9 — "the unbeliever... does not have [the Spirit of Christ]." οὐκ ἔχει is likewise static: the unbeliever's lack of the indwelling Spirit is a condition that always exists, not a variable or occasional state.

Tendential Present

Describes an action or state that is intended or purposed but not yet fully realized.

Romans 2:4 — "Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness... not knowing that God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance?" ἄγει ("leads," from ἄγω) is a tendential present active indicative: it describes an action intended and tending toward reality without necessarily achieving it — the kindness of God has the intent of bringing a person to repentance while preserving genuine human volition.

Romans 5:1 — "we have peace with God" (subjunctive reading: "let us have peace/prosperity"). ἔχωμεν is a hortatory exhortation toward a state not yet fully possessed, not an assertion that the state already obtains.

Romans 7:14 — "I am of the flesh, sold under sin." εἰμί ("I am") is a tendential present: it describes what is inclined toward realization but is not actually, presently occurring. A carnal believer could not write Scripture — Paul's use of the first person here is objective and representative, articulating the universal vulnerability of every believer, not a personal confession of ongoing carnal status.

Progressive Present

Describes an action in process, ongoing at the time in view.

Romans 1:14 — "I am under obligation both to Greeks and to barbarians." The retroactive progressive present of εἰμί (ὀφειλέτης εἰμί, "I am a debtor") describes Paul's obligation as a permanent characteristic of his matured soul, begun at his own maturity adjustment and continuing without interruption as his way of life — not a temporary resolve but a continuous present reality.

Romans 2:1 — "for in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things." πράσσεις ("you practice," from πράσσω) is a retroactive progressive present: what began in the past and continues — self-righteous judgment is not an isolated act but a characteristic pattern of life.

Romans 2:5 — "But because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself." θησαυρίζεις ("you are storing up," from θησαυρίζω) is a retroactive progressive present: begun at the point of maladjustment and continuing to the present — the self-righteous man believes he is building a defense; he is building his own indictment.

Romans 2:9 — "There will be tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil." The articular present middle participle of κατεργάζομαι ("bring about, produce") is a retroactive progressive present, begun at the moment of negative volition at gospel hearing and continuing — inner content working its way to outward behavior.

Romans 2:10 — "but glory and honor and peace for everyone who does good." The articular present middle participle of ἐργάζομαι ("accomplish") is likewise a retroactive progressive present, describing the mature believer who has cracked the maturity barrier and sustains active accomplishment of the good.

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind." εἶναι (the infinitive of εἰμί, "to be") functions with a progressive present force in the accusative-plus-infinitive construction: a persistent, continuing state — the legalist claims to be, and continues to be, a guide and a light.

Romans 3:22 — "the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe." The articular present active participle πιστεύοντας ("those who believe," from πιστεύω) is a retroactive progressive present: an action begun in the past — at the fall of man — and continuing to the present, indicating the universal, unchanging scope of salvation through faith.

Romans 4:4 — "to the one who works." ἐργαζομένῳ ("who works") is a present participle describing action in process.

Perfective Present

Denotes the continuation of existing results into the present, stressing durative authority or force.

Romans 1:17 — "For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith." ἀποκαλύπτεται ("is revealed," from ἀποκαλύπτω) is a perfective present: the justice of God has been revealed in the past — at the cross, in the Mosaic covenant, in the prophetic tradition — and those results are now a present, active reality, comparable to English "I learned" functioning as "I have learned, and it stands."

Romans 1:27 — "men committing shameless acts with men and receiving in themselves the due penalty." κατεργαζόμενοι ("accomplishing," from κατεργάζομαι) functions as a progressive or perfective present participle: action begun in the past and continuing, or the continuation of existing results — the volitional, deliberate production of the act from an inwardly motivated lust.

Romans 2:1 — the same verse's κατακρίνεις ("you condemn," from κατακρίνω) is a perfective present: the self-condemnation came to be at the moment of judging, and its consequences persist.

Romans 3:31 — "Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law." ἱστάνομεν ("we establish," from ἱστάνω, an Attic verb distinct from ἵστημι though related) is a perfective present: the continuation of existing results — through faith in Christ, the believer establishes the true purpose of the law.

Romans 4:6 — "David also speaks." λέγει ("speaks") — the Psalms, composed in the past, remain permanently authoritative and quotable in the present.

Romans 4:12 — "who also walk in the footsteps." The perfective present of "walk" (see section XXI's vocabulary entry on στοιχέω) denotes continuation of existing results through daily doctrine intake: sustained, unbroken forward movement, not a single act.

Romans 5:2 — "let us rejoice" (ESV) / "let us boast." καυχώμεθα ("let us boast," present middle subjunctive of καυχάομαι) is a perfective present: the boasting came into existence in the past, as the believer grasped the integrity of God and eternal security, with the result now emphasized as ongoing.

Romans 5:3 — "we rejoice in our sufferings" (ESV) / "let us boast in our sufferings." καυχώμεθα again, perfective present: not a momentary response but a sustained disposition produced by resident doctrine.

Romans 6:3 — "Do you not know...?" ἀγνοεῖτε ("are you ignorant") is a perfective present: a condition of ignorance that came to exist in the past and continues as a present reality.

Romans 6:6 — "We know that our old self was crucified with him." γινώσκοντες ("knowing," a present active participle) is a perfective present, denoting the continuation of existing results — specifically the results of having perceived retroactive and current positional truth.

Romans 7:16 — "I agree with the law, that it is good." σύμφημι ("I agree") is a perfective present indicating a settled, ongoing agreement — the believer concurs with the law's excellence, not as a return to it as a standard of justification, but as a recognition of its defining function.

Romans 7:19 — "I do the very thing I hate" (the parallel clause). πράσσω ("I keep on practicing") is a perfective present denoting the continuation of existing results: an ongoing pattern of practicing evil that follows from sincerity-driven decision-making without doctrinal policy.

Customary Present

Denotes what habitually or characteristically occurs, without reference to a single instance.

Romans 1:18 — "who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth." κατεχόντων ("suppress," from κατέχω, a compound of κατά + ἔχω) is a customary present participle: the characteristic, habitual activity of the person in maladjustment, not a single act of rejection — each act deepens the pattern and accelerates the darkening described in verses 21–22.

Romans 1:20 — "For his invisible attributes... have been clearly perceived." καθορᾶται ("are clearly perceived," from καθοράω) is a customary present passive indicative: what habitually, invariably occurs when a normal human mind engages the created order at the point of God-consciousness.

Romans 2:7 — "to those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality." ζητοῦσιν ("seek," from ζητέω) is a customary present participle: what habitually characterizes those positive at God-consciousness — a two-stage sequence of seeking and finding.

Romans 2:23 — "You who boast in the law dishonor God by breaking the law." καυχᾶσαι ("you boast," a deponent from καυχάομαι) is a customary present: the habitual, ongoing boast of the legalist who turns the law into self-promotion.

Romans 3:26 — "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." The customary present tense of δικαιόω in this verse indicates invariable repetition: God performs the identical judicial act of justification for every person who believes, without exception, without degrees.

Romans 2:1 — "you who judge practice the very same things." The articular present active participle of κρίνω ("judge") is temporal with a customary-present force: habitual action, every time you judge.

Romans 3:21 — "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it." The circumstantial participle of μαρτυρέω ("bear witness") is a present tense indicating the perpetual validity of the Old Testament's canonical witness to divine integrity.

Romans 5:3 — "suffering produces endurance." κατεργάζεται ("produces") is a customary present: it denotes what habitually occurs in a mature believer under pressure, not a single event.

Romans 5:11 — "we also rejoice in God." καυχώμενοι ("rejoicing/boasting," a participle) is a customary present, denoting what habitually characterizes mature believers.

Romans 7:16 — "I do what I do not want." ποιῶ — descriptive present active indicative, confirming that the described action is a real, ongoing pattern of experience, not a hypothetical.

Romans 7:21 — "when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand." θέλω ("I wish/desire") is used as a customary present in the dative absolute construction describing a recurring resolve (see the Dative Absolute entry, section XIV).

Romans 8:28 — "all things work together for good." συνεργεῖ is a customary present: what habitually occurs in the life of the mature believer specifically — this is not a universal promise applicable to every believer in every condition, but the exclusive, habitual experience of the one who has cracked the maturity barrier.

Romans 8:37 — "we are more than conquerors." ὑπερνικῶμεν is a customary present, indicating what habitually occurs when the justice of God imputes dying blessing to the mature believer through undeserved suffering or martyrdom.

Descriptive Present

A present tense that simply reports an action as an actual, ongoing occurrence, without the additional nuances (durative force, habitual pattern) carried by the perfective or customary present.

Romans 2:3 — "Do you suppose, O man... that you will escape the judgment of God?" λογίζῃ ("you suppose," from λογίζομαι) is a descriptive present depicting an ongoing, characteristic mental activity — shallow self-serving presumption, not careful reasoning.

Romans 1:16 — "For I am not ashamed of the gospel." ἐπαισχύνομαι (negated, "I am ashamed," a compound of ἐπί + αἰσχύνομαι) is present middle indicative describing the ongoing mental attitude of the mature believer: not merely absence of shame but positive elevation above it, since shame is the signature of reversionism.

Romans 3:4 — "By no means! Let God be true though every one were a liar." γινέσθω ("let it be," a present middle imperative of γίνομαι) stands in place of εἰμί precisely because God does not "become" anything in himself but is apprehended as reliable as doctrine reshapes the believer's thinking. Indirect middle voice: God is the agent producing the action of self-demonstration.

Romans 3:24 — "and are justified by his grace as a gift." The present passive participle of δικαιόω ("justify") emphasizes that justification is bestowed upon the believing sinner, not achieved — the passive voice itself carries the doctrine.

Romans 7:16 — "I do what I do not want." ποιῶ confirms that doing what one does not desire is an ongoing, real experience — the plainest, most direct reporting use of the present tense in this passage.

Romans 7:20 — "if I do what I do not want." Same verb, same plain-descriptive force.

Romans 8:6 — "to set the mind on the flesh is death." The two present-tense verbal nouns describing the mind's orientation function descriptively, reporting the actual, ongoing mental activity of each category of person without additional durative or habitual emphasis beyond the plain report.

Aoristic Present

A present tense used to describe a punctiliar, decisive act occurring now, rather than a process or ongoing state — the present-tense counterpart to the aorist's momentary aspect.

Romans 6:11 — "consider yourselves dead to sin." λογίζεσθε ("consider," present middle imperative of λογίζομαι) is an aoristic present: a decisive act of concluding, reached now, not a process spread over time.

Historical Present

Views a past event with the vividness of a present occurrence.

Romans 4:10 — "Was it before or after he had been circumcised?" ὄντι describes Abraham's past state with narrative vividness.

Romans 4:17 — "who gives life to the dead." ζωοποιοῦντος ("who gives life") describes God's past act of reviving Abraham and Sarah's sexual function, with the vividness of a present event.

Romans 4:17 — "and calls into existence the things that do not exist." καλοῦντος and both occurrences behind "do not exist... into existence" describe the naming of what did not exist as existing, and its restoration, both with narrative vividness.

Romans 7:21 — "So I find it to be a law." εὑρίσκω ("I discover/find") in the historical present conveys discovery with present vividness: Paul is not merely reporting a past finding but presenting the principle as immediately operative for every believer reading the text.

Romans 8:16 — "the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit." συμμαρτυρεῖ is a historical present carrying perfective force (see Perfect-as-Present, section II).

Futuristic Present

Describes a future event as though already certain and therefore already in view.

Romans 2:16 — "on that day when, according to my gospel, God judges the secrets of men." κρίνει ("judges," from κρίνω) is a futuristic present: an event not yet occurred but expressed as present because of its absolute certainty.

Romans 2:27 — "Then he who is physically uncircumcised but keeps the law will condemn you." The present active participle of τελέω ("to complete, accomplish") functioning as a conditional protasis is a futuristic present: an event regarded as so certain that the present tense treats it as already occurring.

Romans 4:24 — "It will be counted to us." μέλλει (behind "it will be") treats the imputation of righteousness to future believers — not yet occurred at the time of writing — as certain and therefore already in view.

Retroactive Progressive Present

Describes a condition that began in the past and continues, unbroken, into the present moment.

Romans 4:17 — "the things that do not exist." μὴ ὄντα describes Abraham's sexual death as begun years before and continuing unbroken into the moment of the promise.

Romans 6:8 — "we believe that we will also live with him." πιστεύομεν ("we believe") is retroactive progressive: doctrine perceived in the past continues to be operative in the present because notional knowledge (γνῶσις) has been converted to full, exact knowledge (ἐπίγνωσις) through the non-meritorious perception link of faith. This is the daily function of GAP, not initial saving faith.

Romans 7:17 — "sin that dwells within me." οἰκοῦσα (present participle of οἰκέω) is a retroactive progressive present confirming the sin nature's permanent indwelling: the action began at birth and continues uninterrupted through the entire course of physical life, including life after salvation.

Romans 7:20 — same construction, same force: οἰκοῦσα ἁμαρτία, "sin that dwells within me" — the sin nature is not eradicated, suppressed, or diminished at the new birth.

Romans 7:21 — "evil lies close at hand." παράκειμαι ("to be present, to reside") in the retroactive progressive present indicates that evil has resided in the believer since the moment Adam's sin was imputed at birth, continuing to the present.

Romans 8:5 — "those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh." φρονοῦσιν (present active indicative of φρονέω) is a retroactive progressive present: what has been occurring in the past and continues into the present — the carnal or reversionistic believer keeps thinking the things of the old sin nature, a settled and continuing orientation, not an occasional lapse.

Romans 8:9 — "you are not in the flesh but in the Spirit." ἐστέ (negated) is a retroactive progressive present: a condition begun at the moment of salvation and continuing without interruption — indeed permanently — into the present.

Pictorial Present

Presents a scene as a mental photograph of an ongoing condition.

Romans 4:19 — "since he was about a hundred years old." ὑπάρχων presents Abraham's persisting condition as a single mental image.

Romans 6:16 — the present tense of παρίστημι ("present/place yourselves," discussed fully at 6:13) is pictorial: it brings before the mind the ongoing, daily process of self-placement under orders — a repeated volitional act, not a single decision. Salvation is once-for-all; the authority decision is renewed daily.

Present of Duration

Denotes a condition that began in the past and continues, without break, up to the present moment.

Romans 1:28 — "God gave them up to a debased mind to do what ought not to be done." The articular present active participle τὰ μὴ καθήκοντα ("those things not proper," from καθήκω negated) denotes duration: what was begun in the past and continues into the present — the worthless mind keeps on doing what is improper.

Romans 6:16 — "you are slaves of the one whom you obey." ἐστε (present active of εἰμί) is a present of duration: the believer has been exercising a daily option since the moment of salvation, and that pattern of exercise is the reality being described.

Iterative Present

Describes an action recurring at successive instances, not a single occurrence covering all cases at once.

Romans 2:15 — "They show that the work of the law is written on their hearts." ἐνδείκνυνται ("show, demonstrate," from ἐνδείκνυμι) is an iterative present, indirect middle: what recurs at successive intervals of history — across every generation, Gentiles without the law have repeatedly, observably demonstrated this fact.

Romans 4:24 — "to whom it will be counted." λογίζεσθαι describes the imputation recurring at each instance of a person believing, not once for the whole race collectively.

Romans 7:25 — "with my mind I myself serve the law of God." δουλεύω ("I serve") is iterative — describing what occurs at successive intervals, the alternative resulting from an ongoing, repeated option, not a single decision.

Nomic Future

A future-tense form used not to predict a single event but to state what may rightly be expected under stated conditions — an academic, classificatory statement rather than a prophecy. (Treated fully in section V.)

Romans 7:3 — "she will be called an adulteress" (ESV). χρηματίσει ("she shall be classified as") is a nomic future: a purely objective, legal-classificatory statement, not a moral denunciation.

Romans 8:32 — "he... will he not also graciously give us all things?" χαρίσεται functions simultaneously as a nomic future and a deliberative future (see section V) depending on the hearer's spiritual condition — a genuinely dual-purpose use of a single future-tense form.

IV.Verbal Aspect — the Imperfect Tense

Imperfect of Duration

Contemplates an action or state as having been continuously in progress over a period of past time, without asserting that the process is now complete.

Romans 5:13 — "sin indeed was in the world before the law was given." ἦν ("was," imperfect active of εἰμί) is an imperfect of duration: sin was in continuous existence throughout the period from Adam to Moses. Paul's point is that this ongoing state did not depend on the Mosaic law's existence — the law did not introduce sin, it only defined and declared it.

Romans 7:6 — "having died to that which held us captive." κατειχόμεθα (imperfect passive of κατέχω, "to hold down, suppress, bind") emphasizes that the tyranny of the sin nature as first husband was continuous from physical birth to the new birth. The passive voice establishes that this bondage was received, not chosen.

Romans 7:9 — "I was once alive apart from the law." ἔζων (imperfect of ζάω) is an imperfect of duration: the condition of living apart from the law was an ongoing state in past time — for Paul writing the letter it belongs to the past, but for a reader not yet instructed it may still describe a present condition, so the tense marks both the historical character of the experience and its continuing applicability to anyone not yet exposed to the law's diagnosis.

Romans 8:3 — "the law, weakened by the flesh." ἠσθένει (imperfect active of ἀσθενέω, "to be weak") is a progressive imperfect describing an ongoing condition in past time: the law was continually weak — not a single failure but a sustained state of powerlessness, owing entirely to the flesh (the sin nature) it had to contend with, not to any defect in the law itself.

Inceptive Imperfect

Denotes linear, ongoing past action viewed from its starting point, without a stated point of completion.

Romans 6:17 — "you who were once slaves of sin." ἦτε ("you kept on being," imperfect active of εἰμί) is an inceptive imperfect representing linear, ongoing past action without a stated endpoint. The positional termination of slavery to the sin nature is salvation; the experiential termination depends entirely on the believer's volitional engagement with doctrine.

V.Verbal Aspect — the Future Tense

Romans 2:6 — "He will render to each one according to his works." ἀποδώσει ("will render," from ἀποδίδωμι) is a predictive future active indicative: Christ as presiding judge (John 5:22) produces this proportional rendering — commensurate reward or punishment, quoted from Psalm 62:12 and Proverbs 24:12.

Romans 2:12 — "All who have sinned without the law will also perish without the law, and all who have sinned under the law will be judged by the law." κριθήσονται ("will be judged," from κρίνω) is a future passive, gnomic in force: a fact rightly expected under the conditions of Jewish maladjustment — the passive voice shows the judgment coming from an external standard (the law), contrasted with the middle-voice ἀπολοῦνται ("will perish") for the Gentile, whose perishing reflects his own condition.

Romans 2:26 — "So, if a man who is uncircumcised keeps the precepts of the law, will not his uncircumcision be regarded as circumcision?" λογισθήσεται ("will be regarded/credited," from λογίζομαι) is a future passive indicative in a rhetorical question introduced by the negative οὐκ, requiring the answer "yes" — a key lexeme carried forward into Romans 4:3–5.

Romans 3:3 — "Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God?" καταργήσει ("will nullify," from καταργέω) is a deliberative future: shall their lack of faith cancel the integrity of God? The expected answer is a categorical no.

Romans 3:6 — "For then how could God judge the world?" A deliberative future underlies this rhetorical question, posed from within a debater's technique that Paul immediately overturns.

Predictive Future

Anticipates an event as certain to occur in future time.

Romans 5:7 — "one will scarcely die for a righteous person." ἀποθανεῖται (future middle of ἀποθνῄσκω) anticipates the event; the middle voice here is permissive — the agent voluntarily yields himself to the result of the action, as a soldier might fall on a grenade or a person might die from a sense of duty.

Romans 5:9 — "we shall be saved by him from the wrath of God." σωθησόμεθα (future passive of σῴζω) prophesies ultimate sanctification — the resurrection body — as a future certainty. The passive voice indicates the believer receives this: it is given, not earned.

Romans 5:10 — "we shall be saved by his life." The same verb, σωθησόμεθα, describing the believer's future deliverance in Christ's resurrection life.

Romans 5:17 — "those who receive... will reign in life." βασιλεύσουσιν (future active of βασιλεύω) is predictive: it indicates what is expected in future time, specifically eternity — the reward of rulership reserved for the mature believer, not a present condition.

Romans 5:19 — "the many will be made righteous." κατασταθήσονται (future passive of καθίστημι) is a predictive future, anticipating the imputation of divine righteousness to every future believer.

Romans 6:14 — "sin will have no dominion over you." κυριεύσει (future active of κυριεύω) combines with the negative to state a predictive future functioning as an obligation: the sin nature will not lord it over the believer — provided the believer consistently uses the grace provisions available.

Romans 7:24 — "Who will deliver me from this body of death?" ῥύσεται (future of ῥύομαι) is predictive of an ongoing rescue rather than a single dramatic event (see the Progressive Future entry immediately below).

Romans 8:11 — "he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies." A straightforward predictive future describing the resurrection of the believer's body as a future certainty, guaranteed by the same Spirit who raised Christ.

Romans 8:30 — the entire "golden chain" of verbs (foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified) is stated in the aorist, not the future, even though glorification is not yet experientially complete for any living believer — a deliberate grammatical choice (see the Gnomic Aorist entry, section I): in the divine decrees, all five steps are already, in effect, accomplished fact.

Progressive Future

A future tense describing an action that will unfold gradually, day by day, rather than occurring as a single completed event.

Romans 7:24 — "Who will deliver me from this body of death?" ῥύσεται (future of ῥύομαι, "to rescue," specifically a rescue by dragging someone out of danger when they cannot move under their own power) is a progressive future: the rescue proceeds day by day through consistent doctrinal intake, not through a single dramatic experience. The reversionistic believer cannot self-rescue — the verb itself establishes complete helplessness.

Deliberative Future

A future-tense verb used in a rhetorical question, standing in for a direct assertion — the question admits only one correct answer.

Romans 6:1 — "What shall we say then?" The opening formula employs a deliberative future of λέγω, framing the rhetorical question that opens the chapter.

Romans 6:2 — "How can we who died to sin still live in it?" ζήσομεν is a deliberative future: the rhetorical question functions as an assertion by means of a question that admits only one answer — we cannot.

Romans 8:32 — see the Nomic Future entry above: χαρίσεται functions as a deliberative future for the believer who has already cracked the maturity barrier, for whom the blessing described is already a present reality rather than a future hope.

Romans 8:33 — "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect?" ἐγκαλέσει (future active of ἐγκαλέω, "to bring charges, to accuse, to institute judicial proceedings") is a deliberative future: a rhetorical question stated in the future indicative to make a doctrinal assertion without direct declaration — no creature, whether Satan or a fellow believer, has any authorization to bring such an accusation.

Romans 8:35 — "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" The future of χωρίζω here functions identically: a rhetorical question expecting the answer "no one," not a genuine inquiry about a future event.

Imperative Future

A future-tense verb used to express a command involving futurity — well attested in classical Greek, and paralleling (though not derived from) the Hebrew idiom of the negative plus imperfect as a prohibition.

Romans 7:7, quoting Exodus 20:17 — "You shall not covet" (ESV) / "You will not lust." οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις (future active of ἐπιθυμέω with the negative οὐκ) is an imperative future: the tenth commandment issued as a future-tense prohibition rather than a present-tense one, a construction with genuine classical precedent, not merely a Hebraism.

VI.Mood

Indicative — Declarative vs. Potential Use

The indicative is the mood of reality, but within a debater's argument it can present a premise potentially — exploring what follows from an assumption without asserting the assumption itself.

Romans 4:2 — "For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God." The indicative ἐδικαιώθη here is used potentially, within a debater's-technique first-class condition (see section IX): the premise is assumed for the sake of argument, then shown to lead to an impossible conclusion (boasting before God), and therefore rejected.

Romans 4:14 — "For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be heirs, faith is null and void." Same potential-indicative-within-debater's-technique structure.

Romans 4:17 — "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist." Here the indicative participles are declarative, not potential: they state, as settled fact, the character of God's action.

Romans 7:24 — "Who will deliver me?" The interrogative indicative mood here signals that an answer exists even as the question is being asked — the answer, developed fully in Romans 8, is the Holy Spirit plus Bible doctrine resident in the soul.

The Subjunctive of Emphatic Negation — οὐ μή

The double negative οὐ μή with the subjunctive is the strongest possible negation available in Greek.

Romans 4:8 — "blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin." οὐ μὴ λογίσηται — the double negation with the aorist subjunctive states categorically and permanently that the Lord will never, under any circumstance, hold this sin against the believer's account.

Hortatory Subjunctive

Includes the speaker with the audience in a course of action not yet undertaken.

Romans 5:1 — "we have peace with God" (subjunctive reading, "let us have prosperity"). ἔχωμεν — the omega ending marks this as subjunctive, not indicative (a critical textual and grammatical distinction: the omicron form ἔχομεν would be declarative "we have," but the best manuscript evidence and internal logic favor the hortatory ἔχωμεν) — Paul exhorts believers, himself included, toward exploitation of the peace already established.

Romans 5:2 — "let us rejoice" (ESV, "we rejoice"). καυχώμεθα is likewise hortatory: an exhortation, not a flat assertion of present fact.

Romans 5:3 — "let us rejoice in our sufferings" (ESV, "we also rejoice"). Same hortatory force, extending the exhortation into the realm of undeserved suffering.

Potential Subjunctive

A subjunctive expressing an action or result qualified by contingency — the outcome depends on a condition being met, not stated as an unconditional certainty.

Romans 6:4 — "in order that... we too might walk in newness of life." περιπατήσωμεν (potential subjunctive) establishes newness of life as a potential, not an automatic reality — converted into actuality only through the capacity factor of doctrine resident in the soul.

Romans 6:6 — "so that the body of sin might be brought to nothing." καταργηθῇ (potential subjunctive with ἵνα) is likewise qualified by contingency, pointing toward the believer's future perception and application of retroactive positional truth.

Romans 7:2 (protasis of the third-class condition) — the subjunctive mood of the protasis "if her husband dies" is potential, appropriate to a condition of probability (see Conditional Sentences, section IX).

Romans 8:4 — "in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us." πληρωθῇ is a potential subjunctive: not all believers necessarily advance to the maturity this verse describes; the potential depends on two consistent functions — rebound as needed, and the daily function of GAP.

Potential Indicative

Conveys obligation, impulse, or possibility rather than flat assertion, often with an indefinite subject.

Romans 5:7 — "for one will scarcely die for a righteous person." The construction with τις and the future middle conveys a soldier's instinctive impulse or duty — an indefinite subject acting from impulse rather than a specific, named agent acting from calculated obligation.

Romans 6:14 — "sin will have no dominion over you" — functions as a potential indicative of obligation: what the believer is obligated to experience given the design of grace, provided the grace provisions are used.

Romans 7:20 — "if I do what I do not want." θέλω with the negative here is a potential indicative establishing that the desire not to follow the sin nature's trends is realizable only through doctrine.

Romans 7:25 — the two options of the closing correlative (see section X) are respectively cast as a potential indicative of obligation (serving God's law with the mind) and a potential indicative of impulse (serving sin's law with the flesh) — the same grammatical category, obligation versus impulse, distinguishing principled choice from reactive drift.

The Optative of Wishing — μὴ γένοιτο

The optative mood expresses a wish; combined with the strong negative μή, this fixed idiom is the strongest negative denial available in Koine Greek.

Romans 6:2 — "By no means!" μὴ γένοιτο rejects the hypothetical premise (that grace could license continued sin) with total, categorical force.

Romans 6:15 — "By no means!" Same fixed idiom, rejecting the same category of hypothetical at a later point in the argument.

Romans 7:7 — "By no means!" μὴ γένοιτο again, categorically repudiating the hypothetical conclusion that the law itself is sin — the aorist optative of γίνομαι combined with μή constitutes the strongest available refutation available in the language, a total rejection of the premise, not a qualified disagreement.

Romans 7:13 — "By no means!" A third occurrence, rejecting the suggestion that the good law itself became the cause of spiritual death to the believer.

The Imperatival Participle

A participle that functions as a command while retaining its participial form.

Romans 6:6 — "We know that our old self was crucified with him." γινώσκοντες functions imperativally: the participle carries the force of a command to recognize and apply this fact, not merely to note it in passing.

Direct Middle Voice (Reflexive)

The middle voice where the action returns directly to the subject.

Romans 6:11 — "consider yourselves dead to sin." λογίζεσθε (present middle imperative) is a direct middle: the believer performs the action of reckoning upon himself, the subject and object of the action being one and the same.

Indirect Middle Voice

The middle voice used to indicate the action's spontaneous production by the subject, without necessarily returning the action directly to the subject as its own object (contrast the direct middle above).

Romans 7:9's ἀνέζησεν ("sin came alive," aorist indicative of ἀναζάω) functions with an indirect-middle-like force even though formally active: the sin nature itself is presented as the spontaneous producer of its own activation, not merely a passive recipient of the commandment's arrival.

Participles — Circumstantial and Articular

A circumstantial participle describes the attending condition of an action without itself being the main verb; an articular participle takes the definite article and functions substantivally.

Romans 4:4 — "to the one who works" — ἐργαζομένῳ is an articular participle functioning substantivally, "the one working."

Romans 4:5 — "believes in him who justifies the ungodly" — δικαιοῦντα, an articular participle naming God by his characteristic action.

Romans 4:12 — "who also walk in the footsteps" — a circumstantial participle describing the attending condition of genuine faith.

Romans 4:20–21 — "he grew strong... being fully convinced" — πληροφορηθείς is a circumstantial participle describing the condition under which Abraham's growth occurred.

Romans 5:1 — "since we have been justified" — δικαιωθέντες, a circumstantial participle giving the ground for the exhortation that follows.

Romans 5:10 — "while we were enemies" — ὄντες, a circumstantial participle describing the condition of the human race at the moment reconciliation occurred.

Romans 5:11 — "through whom we have now received reconciliation" — a circumstantial construction describing the means by which believers now stand.

Romans 6:9 — "We know that Christ, being raised from the dead" — ἐγερθείς, a circumstantial aorist participle.

Romans 7:21 — "when I want to do right." The articular present active participle of θέλω functions substantivally within a dative absolute construction (see section XIV): "to me, the one wishing to do the honorable."

Romans 8:37 — "him who loved us." The articular aorist active participle of ἀγαπάω functions substantivally, "the one who loves us" (see the Gnomic Aorist entry, section I).

VII.Voice

The Divine Passive

The passive voice used where the agent is God, left grammatically implicit but doctrinally unmistakable.

Romans 2:25 — Circumcision material throughout uses the divine passive pattern consistently seen across Romans (see the Divine Passive entries in Romans 4–8): the believer receives; he does not produce.

Romans 1:1 — "set apart for the gospel of God." The perfect passive participle ἀφωρισμένος is passive: Paul receives the appointment; God executes it (see also Perfect Tense).

Romans 1:4 — "and was declared to be the Son of God in power... by his resurrection from the dead." The passive voice of ὁρισθέντος ("having been declared") shows Christ receiving the appointment made in eternity past, not claiming it for himself.

Romans 4:2 — "if Abraham was justified by works" — ἐδικαιώθη, passive, with God as the implicit agent (hypothetically, in this debater's-technique case).

Romans 4:3 — "it was counted to him" — ἐλογίσθη, passive, God the implicit crediting agent.

Romans 4:7 — "blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven" — ἀφέθησαν, passive, God the forgiving agent.

Romans 4:9 — "we say that faith was counted to Abraham" — ἐλογίσθη, passive.

Romans 4:10 — "How then was it counted to him?" — ἐλογίσθη, passive.

Romans 4:14 — "faith is null and void" — κεκένωται, passive.

Romans 4:20 — implicit throughout the account of Abraham's strengthening — the passive constructions consistently locate God, not Abraham, as the producer of the spiritual growth described.

Romans 4:21 — "fully convinced" — πληροφορηθείς, passive, the conviction produced in Abraham by an external, divine agent.

Romans 4:22 — "his faith was counted to him as righteousness" — ἐλογίσθη, passive.

Romans 4:25 — "who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" — παρεδόθη and ἠγέρθη, both passive, God the Father the implicit agent throughout.

Romans 5:1 — "we have been justified" — δικαιωθέντες, passive.

Romans 5:5 — "God's love has been poured into our hearts" — ἐκκέχυται, passive, though here the agent (God) is explicit rather than merely implicit, reinforcing the same theological pattern.

Romans 5:9 — "we have now been justified" — δικαιωθέντες, passive.

Romans 5:10 — "we were reconciled to God" — κατηλλάγημεν, passive.

Romans 5:19 — "the many will be made righteous" — κατασταθήσονται, passive.

Romans 6:3 — "all of us who have been baptized" — ἐβαπτίσθημεν, passive.

Romans 6:4 — "we were buried therefore with him" — συνετάφημεν, passive.

Romans 6:6 — "our old self was crucified with him" — συνεσταυρώθη, passive.

Romans 6:7 — "one who has died has been set free" — δεδικαίωται, passive.

Romans 7:6 — "we are released from the law" — κατηργήθημεν, passive.

Romans 7:14 — the law's spiritual origin is framed throughout by implicit passive constructions consistent with this same pattern: the human subject consistently receives; he does not produce.

Middle Voice

Distinct semantic category, not collapsed into the passive.

Romans 2:17 — "But if you call yourself a Jew and rely on the law." ἐπαναπαύῃ ("rely upon," from ἐπαναπαύω, a compound of ἐπί + ἀναπαύω) is present middle indicative — a dynamic middle emphasizing the subject's own active reliance, his complete dependence on the law as an instrument of salvation.

Romans 3:25 — "whom God put forward as a propitiation." προέθετο (middle voice of προτίθημι) is a middle-indirect voice construction stressing God the Father as the agent producing the public display of Christ as the mercy seat.

Romans 4:8 — "will not count." λογίσηται (aorist middle subjunctive) — the middle voice here carries a nuance of personal interest or involvement distinct from a bare passive rendering.

Romans 7:11 — "sin... deceived me." Note that the main verbs of this verse are active, not middle, which is itself significant: the sin nature is presented as an aggressive external agent acting upon Paul, not merely a force he inadvertently cooperates with.

Deponent Verbs

A deponent verb lacks an active-voice form in Greek but carries active meaning.

Romans 3:9 — "Are we Jews any better off? Not at all." προῃτιασάμεθα is a deponent verb — middle in form, active in meaning — "we have already filed the indictment," pointing back to the case built across Romans 1:18–2:29.

Romans 5:2 — "let us rejoice" (ESV, "we rejoice"). καυχώμεθα is deponent: middle in form, active in meaning — the believer himself is the one boasting.

Romans 5:3 — "let us rejoice in our sufferings." Same deponent verb, καυχώμεθα.

Romans 5:7 — "one will scarcely die" — τολμᾷ (behind the fuller construction) is a deponent verb meaning "to dare," middle in form but active in meaning.

Romans 5:11 — "we also rejoice in God" — καυχώμενοι, the same deponent verb in participial form.

Romans 8:19 — "the creation waits with eager longing." ἀπεκδέχεται (present middle of the deponent ἀπεκδέχομαι) is middle in form but active in meaning: the material universe itself is presented as the one producing the action of waiting.

Romans 8:32 — "he did not spare." ἐφείσατο (middle in form) is likewise deponent: God the Father, not a passive recipient, produces the action of the verb.

Indirect Middle

Emphasizes the agent as the one producing the action for his own interest or from his own capacity.

Romans 1:2 — "which he promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy Scriptures." προεπηγγείλατο ("promised beforehand," from προεπαγγέλλομαι) is an aorist middle indicative — specifically a direct middle: it establishes that every writer of an Old Testament book personally participated in the results of the action, meaning each writer was himself saved and adjusted to the justice of God, and so communicated the gospel not as external information but as personal knowledge. The indirect middle would only show personal interest; the direct middle shows personal participation in the result.

Romans 1:16 — "For I am not ashamed of the gospel." ἐπαισχύνομαι is present middle, an indirect middle: Paul as agent produces the state of unashamedness rather than merely participating in its results.

Romans 3:4 — "Let God be true though every one were a liar." γινέσθω (present middle imperative of γίνομαι) is an indirect middle: God himself is the agent producing the action of self-demonstration through the doctrines that vindicate him — not merely interested in but the active author of that demonstration.

Romans 1:15 — "so I am eager to preach the gospel." The aorist middle infinitive εὐαγγελίσασθαι (from εὐαγγελίζομαι) carries indirect-middle force, emphasizing Paul himself as the agent personally producing the action of proclamation.

Romans 5:2 — "let us rejoice." καυχώμεθα — beyond its deponent classification, the form also carries indirect-middle force: the believer boasts from his own perceived interest in the object of the boast (the hope of the glory of God).

Romans 5:3 — "let us rejoice in our sufferings." Same indirect-middle nuance applied to the more difficult object of boasting.

Permissive Middle

A middle-voice form in which the subject voluntarily yields himself to the result of the verb's action.

Romans 5:7 — "one will scarcely die for a righteous person." ἀποθανεῖται (future middle) — the subject voluntarily yields himself to death; the action is not imposed on him from outside, but consented to and permitted.

VIII.Negation

οὐ — Negation of Objective Fact

Used with the indicative to negate a fact as an absolute reality.

Romans 4:4 — "his wages are not counted as a gift." οὐ λογίζεται.

Romans 4:2 — implicit in the negative conclusion of the debater's-technique argument ("but not before God").

Romans 4:10 — "not after, but before he was circumcised" — the negation establishes the historical fact.

Romans 4:20 — "No unbelief made him waver." οὐ διεκρίθη, negating the objective fact of doubt across Abraham's entire test.

Romans 5:5 — "hope does not put us to shame." οὐ καταισχύνει.

Romans 7:7 — "You shall not covet." οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις with the imperative future (see section V) denies the reality of the alleged action as an absolute prohibition.

Romans 7:15 — "I do not understand my own actions." οὐ γινώσκω — a plain factual negation of comprehension.

Romans 8:9 — "the unbeliever... does not have [the Spirit of Christ]." οὐκ ἔχει.

μή — Negation of Potential or Volitional Action

Used with participles, subjunctives, and infinitives.

Romans 4:5 — implicit throughout the discussion of one who does not work but believes.

Romans 4:17 — "the things that do not exist" — μὴ ὄντα, negating potential/hypothetical existence rather than a flat historical fact.

Romans 4:19 — "He did not weaken in faith." μὴ ἀσθενήσας (see the Aggressive Negative entry, section I).

Romans 5:14 — the negation implicit in describing those "whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam" involves a μή-type qualification of the manner of sinning, not its bare fact.

μηδέ — Compound Negative Prohibition

A stronger form of μή used to prohibit an ongoing action while adding a further negated element.

Romans 6:13 — "Do not present your members to sin." μηδέ compounds the prohibition, adding a further negated clause to the preceding one within the same command.

mē with the Present Imperative — Prohibition of an Ongoing Action

Romans 6:12 — "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body." μὴ βασιλευέτω — a present imperative negated by μή, prohibiting an action already in some sense underway, rather than one not yet begun.

οὐ μή + Subjunctive — Emphatic Negation

See section VI above for the full treatment of Romans 4:8's οὐ μὴ λογίσηται.

The Idiom οὐ μόνον δέ... ἀλλὰ καί

"And not only this, but also."

Romans 5:3 — "More than that" (ESV) / "And not only this." οὐ μόνον δέ introduces the idiom, extending the argument beyond the immediately preceding point.

Romans 5:11 — "More than that" (ESV) recurs, extending the argument of reconciliation into the further reality of present rejoicing in God.

οὔτε... οὔτε — The Correlative Negative List

A repeated negative conjunction pairing off items in a catalogue, each pair (or single item) exhaustively denying the possibility of the stated thing accomplishing the action in view.

Romans 8:38–39 — "For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us..." The list is structured as four οὔτε... οὔτε couplets followed by two singles, organized into six categories of challenge (human events in history; angelic powers; time itself; supernatural powers; the spatial extremes of the universe; and, finally, a catch-all covering any remaining created thing). The whole series is introduced by an epexegetical ὅτι ("that"), marking the content of Paul's settled conviction (see the Perfect of Settled Confidence entry, section II) and functioning as the grammatical and rhetorical capstone of the entire chapter.

IX.Conditional Sentences

Greek conditional sentences fall into four classes, each summarized by a single governing concept.

First Class — Reality

Assumes the protasis as true (whether for genuine assertion or, in debater's technique, for the sake of argument).

Romans 2:17–23 — "But if you call yourself a Jew and rely on the law and boast in God... you then who teach others, do you not teach yourself?" This entire span is structured as a single first-class condition: the protasis (vv. 17–20) grants all the legalist's premises as true, and the apodosis (vv. 21–23) then draws from those premises the devastating conclusion that the legalist is guilty of the very things he condemns in others.

Romans 3:3 — "What if some were unfaithful? Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God?" εἰ with the indicative forms a first-class condition: the protasis, "if some did not believe," treats Jewish unbelief as a historical reality assumed true for the argument, with the apodosis then asking — and definitively answering in verse 4 — whether such maladjustment can nullify God's own integrity.

Romans 3:30 — "since God is one." εἴπερ (a compound of εἰ intensified by περ) introduces a first-class condition presenting the unity of the Godhead as established fact, not hypothesis: since the Godhead is one in essence, the justice of God justifies circumcised and uncircumcised alike, by the same faith.

Romans 4:2 — "For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God." A first-class condition used in debater's technique: the premise is assumed true for the sake of the argument, then shown to lead to an impossible result and therefore rejected.

Romans 4:14 — "For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be heirs, faith is null and void." Same debater's-technique use.

Romans 5:10 — "For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life." The established-premise use: the protasis is genuinely true and serves as the basis for an a fortiori inference (see section XI) to the apodosis.

Romans 5:17 — "For if, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace... reign in life." Same established-premise structure supporting a fortiori reasoning.

Romans 6:5 — "For if we have been united with him in a death like his, we shall certainly be united with him in a resurrection like his." εἰ with the indicative treats the protasis as reality: since union with Christ in his death is an established fact, the apodosis follows with certainty.

Romans 7:20 — "Now if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me." The first-class condition here assumes the reality of the inner conflict — this is a real, ongoing experience, not hypothetical.

Romans 8:9 — "assuming that the Spirit of God dwells in you." εἴπερ (a compound of εἰ intensified by περ, "if indeed," "if after all," or "since") introduces a first-class condition affirming, as a settled fact, that the Holy Spirit indwells every Church Age believer — the condition is not introducing genuine doubt.

Romans 8:9 (second clause) — "if in fact anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ" (ESV: "does not belong to him"). A second first-class condition, using the simple εἰ with the indefinite pronoun τις functioning not as a genuine unknown but as a category-specifier — here, the category of the unbeliever.

Romans 8:17 — "if children, then heirs... provided we suffer with him." A first-class condition assumed as reality, grounding the believer's heirship and, conditionally, participation in Christ's sufferings and glory.

Romans 8:31 — "If God is for us, who can be against us?" A first-class condition presenting an established truth (God is, in fact, for us) as the premise for the rhetorical question that follows.

Second Class — Unreality (Contrary to Fact)

Assumes the protasis as false, used to explore what would follow if a known-untrue premise were true. Not yet directly exemplified with a labeled instance anywhere in the source material processed across Romans 4–8 — the category is named and defined in the source manuscript's own framework (stated explicitly at Romans 7:2), so this is a gap in the specific material processed, not in the underlying grammar. Its shape is illustrated elsewhere in Paul's writing (for example, 1 Corinthians 15:17, "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile") — noted here for completeness of the four-class framework only, not incorporated as a Romans example, since it does not occur in Romans.

Third Class — Probability

Uses ἐάν ("if") plus the subjunctive mood, expressing a real element of uncertainty combined with a real element of likelihood — genuinely open, pending some further condition.

Romans 2:25 — "For circumcision indeed is of value if you obey the law, but if you break the law, your circumcision becomes uncircumcision." ἐάν with the subjunctive introduces a third-class condition — future possibility with a genuine element of contingency. The verse's apodosis is placed before its protasis, an inversion reflecting the historical fact that circumcision preceded the Mosaic law.

Romans 7:2–3 — "if her husband dies she is released from the law of her husband... if she lives with another man while her husband is alive, she will be called an adulteress." ἐάν with the subjunctive of ἀποθνῄσκω (v.2) and of γίνομαι (v.3) both introduce third-class protases: the outcome is genuinely contingent on which of two possibilities obtains. Applied to the spiritual analogy in the surrounding chapters, the third-class condition makes the believer's divorce from the sin nature strictly contingent on faith in Christ: if the individual believes, the divorce is accomplished; if not, the first marriage remains in force.

Romans 8:13 — "For if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live." Both halves of this verse present the believer's two live, genuinely open outcomes as contingencies dependent on which pattern of life is actually chosen.

Fourth Class — Possibility (Remote)

Expresses a merely conceivable outcome, with the least degree of expectation of the four classes. Not yet directly exemplified with a labeled instance anywhere in the source material processed across Romans 4–8 — noted here for completeness of the four-class framework only.

X.Comparative Sentences

Distinct from conditional sentences: these draw an analogy between two clauses rather than an inference from a premise.

hōsper... houtōs — "Just as... so also"

Romans 5:21 — "so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life." ὥσπερ... οὕτως draws the formal parallel between the reign of sin and the reign of grace.

houtōs kai — "So also"

Romans 5:19 — "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." οὕτως καί completes the Adam/Christ comparison begun in the first half of the verse.

The Elliptical Comparative — Verbs Supplied from Context

Romans 5:18 — "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men." The second half of the comparison omits its own verb, expecting the reader to supply it from the first half — a deliberate compression that mirrors the tight parallelism Paul is drawing between Adam and Christ.

men... de — "On the One Hand... But on the Other Hand"

The classical formal-contrast construction.

Romans 5:16 — "And the free gift is not like the result of that one man's sin. For the judgment following one trespass brought condemnation, but the free gift following many trespasses brought justification." μέν... δέ structures the contrast between the singular origin of condemnation and the multiple-trespass origin of justification.

Romans 7:12 — "So the law is holy" — the emphatic use of μέν alone (without an immediately paired δέ in this instance) functions as "as a matter of fact, in fact," a strong inferential affirmation rather than the full two-sided contrast.

Romans 7:25 — "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin." A full, deliberately symmetrical μέν... δέ construction — pure classical Attic style — presenting the believer's two live options with equal grammatical weight, leaving the choice to volition: serving God's law with the mind (an iterative present, an obligation) against serving sin's law with the flesh (the verb elided and understood from context, an impulse).

XI.A Fortiori Arguments

If the greater has been accomplished, the lesser follows with stronger reason. The fixed idiom is πολλῷ μᾶλλον ("much more"), though the underlying logic recurs even without the fixed phrase.

Romans 5:9 — "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God." πολλῷ μᾶλλον — if the greater (justification, accomplished at the cost of Christ's blood) has been secured, the lesser (final deliverance from wrath) follows with much greater certainty.

Romans 5:10 — "For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life." Same fixed idiom, same structure: reconciliation while enemies (the harder case) guarantees final salvation now that friendship is established (the easier case).

Romans 5:15 — "much more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many." The a fortiori argument applied to the disproportion between Adam's single sin and Christ's superabounding grace.

Romans 5:17 — "much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ." The greatest of the four a fortiori statements in Romans 5, applying the principle to future reigning in resurrection life.

Romans 8:32 — "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also graciously give us all things?" This is the a fortiori argument in its purest form across all the material processed: the greater gift (the Son, given up to judgment) makes the lesser gift (everything else) certain — stated here as a rhetorical question rather than the fixed πολλῷ μᾶλλον idiom, but identical in logical structure.

Romans 8:4's πληρωθῇ (see section I) is grounded in the same a fortiori logic carried over from Romans 5: the imbalance between the greater (imputed righteousness, already given) and the less (blessing in time, not yet realized) demands resolution, and a fortiori reasoning guarantees that resolution will come.

Romans 8:30's five-step chain is itself a chain of a fortiori inferences: foreknowledge guarantees predestination, which guarantees calling, which guarantees justification, which guarantees glorification — each step following from the prior with the same "how could it be otherwise" force.

XII.Ellipsis, Aposiopesis, and Brachylogy

Ellipsis is the grammatical omission of words the reader is expected to supply from context. Aposiopesis is the specific case where the omitted material must be drawn from the immediately preceding context. Brachylogy is deliberate grammatical compression more broadly.

Romans 5:18 — "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men." The second clause omits its own verb by ellipsis, supplying it from the first clause (see the Elliptical Comparative entry, section X).

Romans 6:5 — "For if we have been united with him in a death like his, we shall certainly be united with him in a resurrection like his." The second half compresses what could be a full parallel clause into a brief echo, trusting the reader to supply the fuller sense from the first half.

Romans 7:2 — "For a married woman is bound by law to her husband while he lives." There is no verb at all in the Greek of this clause (see the Predicate Nominative without a Copula entry, section XIV) — the King James Version's supplied "hath" does not correspond to any word in the original; the predicate adjective ὕπανδρος alone completes the clause grammatically.

Romans 7:25's second clause (the μέν side) elides the verb entirely on the δέ side, understood from the μέν clause by classical convention — Greek routinely omits a verb already established by a parallel clause rather than repeating it.

Romans 8:5 — "but those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit." Paul employs ellipsis here: the verb φρονέω, stated explicitly in the first half of the verse, is left to be supplied in the second half rather than repeated.

XIII.Genitive Absolute

Romans 2:15 — "their conscience also bears witness, and their conflicting thoughts accuse or even excuse them." The genitive absolute construction with συνειδήσεως ("conscience") as its subject stands grammatically independent of the main clause — the Gentile conscience functions as an independent witness alongside, not dependent on, the Mosaic law the Gentile does not possess.

A participial construction grammatically independent of the main clause.

Romans 5:6 — "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly." The temporal clause functions with genitive-absolute-like independence from the main clause that follows.

Romans 5:8 — "but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." Same construction, independently describing the condition of the human race at the time of Christ's death.

Romans 7:3 — "if she lives with another man while her husband is alive." ζῶντος τοῦ ἀνδρός ("the husband living") is a genitive absolute: the genitive singular of ἀνήρ serves as the subject of a genitive participle of ζάω, and the whole construction stands grammatically detached from the main clause — mirroring, in its very detachment, the logical detachment of the hypothetical scenario Paul is describing (a scenario he immediately goes on to rule out) from the actual illustration he intends.

XIV.The Dative Absolute, the Uncopulated Predicate Nominative, and the Nominative Absolute

Dative Absolute

A participial construction using the dative case, functioning much like the genitive absolute but marking the person to or for whom the described condition holds, with temporal force.

Romans 7:21 — "when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand." The phrase behind "when I want" is, more literally, "to me, the one wishing to do the honorable" — a dative absolute: dative singular of ἐγώ ("to me") plus the articular present participle of θέλω. The construction functions temporally: "when I desire." The present active participle here is customary, describing what is expected of a believer as a member of the royal family of God — and the active voice represents Paul as the reversionistic believer for the purpose of personalizing the illustration, not as a description of Paul's own condition at the time of writing.

The Predicate Nominative Without a Copula (Ellipsis of "To Be")

Greek does not require an explicit verb "to be" when a predicate nominative or adjective stands in direct relationship to its subject; the clause is grammatically complete without it.

Romans 7:2 — "a married woman is bound..." — more precisely, the underlying clause "the married woman [is] under the authority of a husband" supplies no verb at all in the Greek; the predicate adjective ὕπανδρος ("under the authority of a husband") alone completes the clause. The King James Version's inserted "hath an husband" supplies a verb (ἔχω) that is not present in the original.

The Nominative Absolute

An adjective or substantive standing alone in the nominative case with no finite verb governing it — an independent, verbless construction that throws grammatical weight onto what follows, more abrupt even than the uncopulated predicate nominative.

Romans 8:3 — "For God has done what the law... could not do." The underlying Greek opens with τὸ ἀδύνατον ("the impossible," or "what was impossible") standing alone in the nominative, neuter, with no finite verb — a rare construction in which an idea is expressed independent of any verbal relationship, its only further specification being a descriptive genitive ("of the law"). The sentence is structurally abrupt, and deliberately so: the suspended opening throws the entire weight of the verse onto the divine action that follows.

The Predicate Nominative with Verbs of Becoming or Being Appointed

Certain verbs (εἰμί, γίνομαι, καθίστημι) take a predicate nominative describing the resulting state.

Romans 5:19 — "the many will be made righteous." δίκαιοι stands as a predicate nominative completing κατασταθήσονται.

Romans 6:16 — "you are slaves of the one whom you obey." δοῦλοι ("slaves") stands as a predicate nominative completing ἐστε.

Romans 7:3 — "she will be called an adulteress." μοιχαλίς stands as a predicate nominative completing χρηματίσει (see the Nomic Future entry, section III), the classificatory verb naming the legal category into which she falls.

Proleptic Word Order

A pronoun or other element placed earlier in the sentence than its normal grammatical position would require, for the sake of emphasis — the element is "anticipated" ahead of where it would otherwise fall.

Romans 8:9 — "You, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit." The second-person pronoun σύ stands in the emphatic, proleptic position — placed first in the Greek sentence for stress — immediately followed by an intensified δέ ("in fact," "indeed"). The combined effect announces a decisive distinction: you — and precisely you as a believer — are categorically different from the unbeliever just described.

XV.The Cognate Accusative

A construction in which the direct object of a verb is drawn from the same root as the verb itself.

Romans 6:10 — "the life he lives he lives to God." ζῇ... ζῇ — the verb "lives" is repeated with a cognate object built from the same root, intensifying the assertion: Christ's life is defined by, and consists entirely of, the very act of living itself in relationship to God, with no remainder given to any other reference point.

XVI.Case Usage

Genitive of Relationship

Romans 1:6 — "you also are among those who are called to belong to Jesus Christ." κλητοί stands as a nominative plural verbal adjective — the key word of the verse — with Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ functioning as a genitive of relationship: election grounded entirely in the person of the Lord.

Ablative of Source

Indicates the origin from which something proceeds.

Romans 1:1 — "set apart for the gospel of God." θεοῦ ("of God") is an ablative of source in the phrase εἰς εὐαγγέλιον θεοῦ: God is the origin from which both the gospel and Paul's appointment proceed.

Romans 2:18 — "and know his will." ἐκ τοῦ νόμου ("from the law") is an ablative of source: the Mosaic law is the fountainhead of the legalist's instruction — Paul disputes not the law's legitimacy but the hermeneutic applied to it.

Romans 2:26 — "the righteous requirements of the law." δικαιώματα stands with an ablative of source from νόμος: "the righteous requirements from the law" — referring to the three adjustments to the justice of God that the law depicts.

Romans 3:25 — "whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith." ἀνοχῇ ("forbearance/clemency," from ἀνοχή) appears with an ablative of source from θεός: the suspension of judgment on pre-cross sins originated in God's deliberate clemency, not in any deficiency of his justice.

Romans 2:29 — "whose praise is not from man but from God." ἐκ θεοῦ ("from God," an ablative/genitive of source) identifies the true Jew's approval as originating in the justice of God rather than human community — the fourth and eternal adjustment, dispensed at the judgment seat of Christ.

Romans 3:2 — "they were entrusted with the oracles of God." λόγια ("oracles," accusative plural, distinct from λόγος) designates the entire Old Testament canon entrusted to Israel, its source specified by an ablative of absolute source from θεός.

Romans 4:2 — the ground of Abraham's (hypothetical) boasting would derive from works as its source.

Romans 4:12 — "the father of the circumcised who are not merely circumcised but who also walk in the footsteps of the faith that our father Abraham had" — the faith in view has Abraham's own faith as its historical source and pattern.

Romans 5:1 — "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" — peace, in this construction, proceeds from Christ as its source.

Romans 6:23 — "the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." Eternal life proceeds from God as its source, in Christ Jesus as its channel.

Romans 8:2 — "the law of the Spirit of life." The phrase "of life" is an ablative singular of source from ζωή, not a genitive: not "the Spirit of life" in a simple possessive sense, but "the Spirit who is the source of eternal life."

Romans 8:9 — "the Spirit of Christ." Likewise an ablative of source (not a genitive of possession): the Holy Spirit does not belong to Christ as property, but proceeds from him — the doctrine of Procession.

Ablative of Means

Romans 4:14 — "if it is the adherents of the law who are to be heirs" — the means by which heirship would be secured, in this rejected hypothesis, is adherence to the law.

Romans 3:26 — the same verse's ἐκ πίστεως ("by faith") is an ablative of means, with the objective genitive Ἰησοῦ specifying Christ as the object: faith's effectiveness derives entirely from its object, not from any quality in the one exercising it.

Romans 1:5 — "through whom we have received grace and apostleship." The relative pronoun following διά is a genitive of means/agency: grace and apostleship were received through the agency of the resurrected Christ named in verse 4.

Romans 5:15 — "much more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many." The means by which the free gift abounds is specifically the grace of Christ.

Romans 5:16 — "the free gift following many trespasses brought justification." The trespasses function, in this construction, as the occasion rather than the means proper, but the underlying ablative-of-means logic governs how the "gift" reaches "many."

Romans 6:17 — "you... have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching to which you were committed." The means by which obedience was produced is doctrine received into the heart.

Instrumental Case

Expresses impersonal means.

Romans 1:29 — "They were filled with all manner of unrighteousness." πάσῃ ἀδικίᾳ is an instrumental of means — by/with all anti-justice — describing the maladjusted soul's total saturation.

Romans 3:24 — "and are justified by his grace as a gift." χάριτι ("by grace") is an instrumental case identifying grace as the means — the standard operating procedure — by which the justice of God administers justification; it is not an emotion or attitude in God.

Romans 5:2 — "through whom we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand." τῇ πίστει — faith is the instrumental means by which access is obtained, not itself the meritorious cause.

Romans 5:9 — "we have now been justified by his blood." τῷ αἵματι — instrumental of means: the blood of Christ is the specific instrument by which justification is secured.

Romans 5:15 — "by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ" — τῇ χάριτι, instrumental of means.

Romans 7:2 — "stands bound to her husband by law." νόμῳ — instrumental of means (Mosaic law as the instrument of the binding).

Romans 7:25 — "with my mind... with my flesh." νοΐ and σαρκί are both instrumental datives of means, naming the two competing instruments through which the believer's daily choice of allegiance is exercised.

Instrumental of Association

A dative form (using the intensive pronoun) denoting the party with whom a shared action or identification occurs.

Romans 6:4 — "we were buried therefore with him by baptism into death." αὐτῷ — the instrumental of association identifying Christ as the one with whom the believer's identification occurs.

Romans 7:3 — "if she lives with another man." The instrumental of association governs ἀνήρ here, though — note — in this occurrence ἀνήρ refers not to the legitimate husband but to a different man entirely (see the vocabulary note on ἀνήρ vs. ἄνθρωπος below), reinforced by the adjective ἕτερος ("another of a different kind").

Dative of Advantage

Indicates the party for whose benefit an action occurs.

Romans 1:14 — "I am under obligation both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to the wise and to the foolish." σοφοῖς functions as a dative of advantage (it is to the mature believer's benefit to continue receiving doctrine) while ἀνοήτοις functions as a dative of disadvantage, referencing the condition of spiritual failure.

Romans 4:16 — "That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring." The promise is secured for the advantage of Abraham's spiritual offspring.

Romans 4:20 — "he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God" — the growth described redounds to God's advantage in the sense that it results in his glorification.

Dative of Reference

Indicates the entity toward which or with respect to which an action is directed.

Romans 5:10 — "we were reconciled to God" — τῷ θεῷ, dative of reference: the reconciliation is with reference to God specifically as the offended party.

Romans 6:2 — "How can we who died to sin still live in it?" τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ, dative of reference: the believer's death is with reference to the sin nature's ruling authority.

Romans 6:10 — "the death he died he died to sin, once for all" — τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ, dative of reference again, marking what the death is with reference to.

Romans 6:11 — "consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God" — both τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ and τῷ θεῷ function as datives of reference, marking the two poles of the believer's reckoning.

Romans 7:4 — "you also have died to the law." τῷ νόμῳ — dative of reference: the believer's death is with reference to the law's authority specifically, not a literal physical death.

Romans 7:25 — the datives τῷ νοΐ and τῇ σαρκί could also be read partly as datives of reference (the sphere in which each service occurs) alongside their instrumental force (the means by which it occurs) — Greek often allows both nuances to sit together in a single dative form.

Dative of Indirect Object

Romans 4:3 — "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness." αὐτῷ, dative of indirect object: the party to whom the crediting is made.

Romans 1:7 — "To all those in Rome who are loved by God and called to be saints." ὑμῖν and, earlier in the verse, πᾶσιν, both function as datives of indirect object: the Roman believers are the designated beneficiaries of a plan they did not initiate — they receive, they do not produce.

Romans 4:22 — "That is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness." Same construction, same indirect-object force.

Romans 7:21 (twice, "to me") — see the Dative Absolute entry, section XIV, for the fuller discussion of this dative's function within that construction.

Genitive of Reference

Marks the category or sphere to which a noun's action or state pertains.

Romans 6:6 — "so that the body of sin might be brought to nothing." τῆς ἁμαρτίας ("of sin") is a genitive of reference, marking the sphere to which the "body" in view pertains — the physical body considered specifically as headquarters of the sin nature.

Descriptive Genitive

Romans 4:12 — "the father of the circumcised" — τῆς περιτομῆς, describing the category of people in view by their defining mark.

Romans 1:29 — "envy, murder, strife..." Ἴριδος ("of strife," a descriptive genitive) opens the catalogue of evil-functions, naming not a single act but a habitual disposition.

Romans 3:1 — "Then what advantage has the Jew?" Ἰουδαίου stands as a descriptive genitive, encompassing both the racial and the national identity of Israel as the fourth race and priest nation — not religious practice alone.

Romans 8:2 — the descriptive genitive of πνεῦμα ("of the Spirit") identifies the Holy Spirit as author and agent of "the principle" (νόμος) named in Romans 8:2.

Objective Genitive

The noun in the genitive functions as the object of the verbal idea contained in the noun it modifies.

Romans 5:5 — "God's love has been poured into our hearts." ἡ ἀγάπη τοῦ θεοῦ can be read with an objective-genitive nuance in some contexts (love directed toward God), though the dominant sense here is subjective (God's own love toward the believer) — the two readings are worth distinguishing precisely because English "of" flattens them together.

Romans 6:16 — "you are slaves... leading to righteousness" — the genitive relationship between slavery and its object.

Romans 7:4 — "the law of her husband" (the underlying analogy at 7:2) uses a genitive that functions objectively with respect to the governing relationship named.

Romans 7:5 — "the passions of sins." παθήματα... ἁμαρτιῶν — the plural genitive of ἁμαρτία with the plural noun παθήματα ("impulses, trends") designates the sinful trends as, in Paul's own marriage analogy, the "children" produced by the first marriage: the trend toward sin, toward human good, and toward evil.

Appositional Genitive

Two nouns placed in a genitive relationship where the second renames or explains the first.

Romans 5:2 — "this grace in which we stand" involves, in the fuller Greek construction behind "access... into this grace," an appositional relationship clarifying exactly what the "access" secured actually consists of.

Genitive of Time (Adverbial)

Marks a distinction of time-category rather than a specific point or duration.

Romans 5:18 — "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men" — the temporal framing of "one trespass" situates the event within a category of time (a single, decisive past occasion) rather than specifying a duration.

Anarthrous Constructions — Quality Over Identity

The absence of the definite article on an otherwise-expected noun emphasizes the quality of the thing named.

Romans 2:17 — "But if you call yourself a Jew." ἐν θεῷ ("about relationship with God") is anarthrous, emphasizing the qualitative perfection of God rather than mere identity, even as Paul exposes the arrogance of invoking the divine name as backdrop for boasting.

Romans 4:5 — "believes in him who justifies the ungodly" — ἀσεβῆ is anarthrous, emphasizing the quality of ungodliness as a category rather than pointing to one specific ungodly individual.

Romans 5:1 — "we have peace with God" — εἰρήνην is anarthrous, emphasizing the quality of the peace in view (prosperity, a settled condition) rather than identifying "the peace" as a single previously-mentioned item.

Romans 6:9 — "death no longer has dominion over him" — θάνατος anarthrous, emphasizing death as a category or power rather than one specific death-event.

Romans 6:13 — "present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life" — the anarthrous nouns here stress the qualitative contrast between the two states rather than identifying particular, previously-named instances of each.

Romans 6:14 — "sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace" — both νόμον and χάριν are anarthrous, stressing the qualitative categories (law as a system, grace as a system) rather than one specific law or one specific gift.

Romans 6:19 — "you presented your members as slaves to impurity and to lawlessness" — the anarthrous nouns stress the qualitative character of the former slavery.

Romans 7:6 — "newness of spirit" (ESV). The anarthrous construction of πνεῦμα ("spirit") with the noun καινότης ("newness") emphasizes the quality of the Holy Spirit — incorruptibility — as the defining characteristic of the new marriage counselor, not merely one particular occurrence of newness.

Romans 7:14 — "sold under sin" (ESV). While not itself anarthrous in every occurrence, ἁμαρτία functions across this chapter primarily as a qualitative category (the sin nature as a governing power) rather than a particular identified act — consistent with its anarthrous or generically-articled use throughout Romans 5–7.

Accusative of General Reference

Romans 4:12 — "the father of the circumcised" involves an accusative-of-reference nuance in the fuller construction describing what category of persons the fatherhood in view concerns.

Romans 2:1 — "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things." σεαυτόν ("yourself") stands in emphatic position as an accusative of general reference — a reflexive pronoun foregrounding self-condemnation as the theological climax of the verse, since the judge pronounces his own verdict.

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind." σεαυτόν functions again as an accusative of general reference, the subject of the infinitive εἶναι, emphasizing that the legalist's frame of reference is himself, not God.

Romans 3:22 — "the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe." πάντας ("all," from πᾶς) in the phrase εἰς πάντας indicates that salvation is universally available to every member of the human race without distinction.

The Predicate Nominative with Verbs of Becoming or Being Appointed

See section XIV above for the fuller treatment introduced by Romans 7's uncopulated predicate nominative construction; Romans 5:19, 6:16, and 7:3 all illustrate this pattern and are treated fully there.

XVII.Prepositions

χωρίς — Apart From

Romans 3:21 — "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law." χωρίς governs the genitive of νόμος as an improper preposition: divine righteousness is disclosed entirely apart from, without reference to, any system of legal works.

ἐκ — Source or Origin

Romans 2:27 — "who am uncircumcised by nature." ἐκ φύσεως (ἐκ + genitive) is used idiomatically for a natural, inborn condition — uncircumcised as a matter of birth, not merely as an unperformed ritual.

Romans 3:20 — "For by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight." ἐξ ἔργων νόμου (ἐκ + genitive) marks the excluded source: not from works of the law — the law's true function is to produce the knowledge of sin, not justification.

Romans 3:26 — "it was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." ἐκ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ (ἐκ + genitive) is an ablative of means: by means of faith, with Jesus as the object specified by the objective genitive.

ὑπέρ — For the Sake Of

Romans 1:5 — "for the sake of his name." ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος αὐτοῦ (ὑπέρ + genitive) marks the ultimate purpose for which grace, apostleship, and doctrinal obedience among the nations all exist.

κατά + Accusative

Means "according to" — but frequently, in Romans 6–8, carries a stronger force than the English preposition suggests.

Romans 1:3 — "concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh." κατὰ σάρκα (κατά + accusative of σάρξ) indicates standard or norm — "according to the standard of humanity" — establishing that Christ's humanity is genuine and complete, not docetic. Contrasted with κατὰ πνεῦμα in the following verse.

Romans 2:2 — "We know that the judgment of God rightly falls... according to truth." κατὰ ἀλήθειαν (κατά + accusative) expresses the norm by which the divine verdict is rendered.

Romans 4:1 — "What then shall we say was gained by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh?" κατὰ σάρκα — according to the flesh, i.e., by natural human descent.

Romans 4:4 — "Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due." The κατά-relationship here (implicit in the surrounding argument) frames wages as strictly proportional to work performed.

Romans 5:6 — "at the right time Christ died for the ungodly." κατὰ καιρόν — according to the appointed time, a fixed point in the divine plan rather than an arbitrary moment.

Romans 8:5 — "those who live according to the flesh." κατὰ σάρκα — the corrected sense is "dominated by the flesh," not merely "in agreement with" or "corresponding to" it. κατά with the accusative here conveys domination and authority: it identifies the governing power-source of the believer's thought life at any given point, not a loose correspondence.

Romans 8:33 — "against God's elect." κατά here shifts to a hostile/adversative sense: accusation directed against a target — a different nuance of the same preposition entirely dependent on context.

διά — Genitive vs. Accusative

The case governs the sense: διά + genitive = means/agency; διά + accusative = cause/ground, never purpose.

Romans 1:8 — "First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for all of you." διά + the genitive is a statement of mechanism: all access to God the Father is mediated through the completed cross-work and continuing high-priestly ministry of Christ — no relationship with God exists independently of him.

Romans 2:12 — "All who have sinned without the law will also perish without the law, and all who have sinned under the law will be judged by the law." διὰ νόμου (διά + genitive) expresses means: judgment reaches the Jew through the specific instrument of the Mosaic law.

Romans 3:22 — "the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ." διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ (διά + genitive) specifies the channel: not law-keeping but faith, through which righteousness reaches the believer.

Romans 3:25 — "whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. This was to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins." διά with the accusative (see also the Case Usage entries above on ἀνοχή) marks the ground of the forbearance shown toward pre-cross sins.

Romans 4:13 — "the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law." διὰ νόμου, genitive, means/agency — the promise's channel of arrival was specifically not the law.

Romans 4:25 — "who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." διά here with the accusative in both clauses marks cause/ground: delivered up because of our trespasses (the ground necessitating the delivering-up), raised because of our justification (the ground necessitating the resurrection) — not "for the purpose of," which would require a different construction.

Romans 5:10 — "we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son." διὰ τοῦ θανάτου, genitive, means: the death itself is the instrument/channel of reconciliation.

Romans 5:19 — "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." διά with the genitive in both halves, means: disobedience and obedience are the respective channels through which each result comes.

Romans 6:4 — "We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life." διὰ τῆς δόξης, genitive, means: the Father's glory is the specific instrument of the resurrection.

Romans 7:4 — "you also have died to the law through the body of Christ." διὰ τοῦ σώματος — genitive, means: the physical, human body of Christ (his literal death) as the means by which the believer's death to the law's authority is accomplished.

Romans 8:3 — "through the flesh." διὰ τῆς σαρκός — genitive, means: the sin nature as the channel through which the law's weakness operated.

Romans 8:37 — "through him who loved us." διά + the genitive of the articular participle of ἀγαπάω — means: God the Father, as the one who loves, is the channel through whom supreme victory is won.

παρά — Sense Shifts Entirely by Case

Romans 4:18 — "In hope he believed against hope." παρ' ἐλπίδα — the ESV and KJV render this "against hope," but the correct sense of παρά plus the accusative here is "beyond hope," not adversarial opposition to hope: Abraham believed beyond the point where ordinary hope could reach, not in defiance of some competing hope.

Romans 1:25 — "who worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator." παρά with the accusative denotes a mutually exclusive comparison — "rather than," not "more than": the creature was chosen to the complete exclusion of the Creator, not merely preferred above him.

ἐπί — Sense Shifts by Case

Romans 4:5 — "believes in him who justifies the ungodly" — the fuller construction elsewhere in this discussion using ἐπί with a case governs the object of belief/trust.

Romans 4:18 — "as he had been told, 'So shall your offspring be.'" The construction governing what the promise rests "upon" uses ἐπί in a locative/basis sense.

Romans 5:14 — "Yet death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam." The reign of death extending "over" a category of persons uses ἐπί in this governing/dominating sense.

πρός + Accusative

Directional/relational.

Romans 4:2 — "he has something to boast about, but not before God." πρὸς θεόν — directional/relational: the boasting is oriented toward, and would need to stand up in relation to, God specifically.

Romans 5:1 — "we have peace with God." πρὸς τὸν θεόν — relational: peace is not merely an internal state but a restored relational orientation toward God.

Romans 5:2 — "through whom we have also obtained access... into this grace." The relational directionality of "access" is governed by a πρός-type construction in the underlying Greek argument.

εἰς + Accusative

Marks predicate result, purpose, or reference.

Romans 1:1 — "set apart for the gospel of God." εἰς εὐαγγέλιον θεοῦ uses εἰς with the accusative to express cause and purpose, not subordination: Paul was appointed because the gospel required an apostle of his specific preparation, not merely placed "under" the gospel.

Romans 4:3 — "it was counted to him as righteousness." εἰς δικαιοσύνην — marks the predicate result: crediting resulting in the status of righteousness.

Romans 4:11 — "he received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith." εἰς σφραγῖδα — marks purpose/result: circumcision given for the purpose of being a seal.

Romans 4:16 — "That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring." εἰς marks the purpose of the arrangement.

Romans 4:20 — "No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God." The reference-marking function of εἰς-type constructions governs what the unwavering faith concerned.

Romans 5:18 — "so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men." εἰς δικαίωσιν ζωῆς — marks result: the one act of righteousness resulting in justification-of-life.

Romans 6:3 — "all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death." εἰς Χριστόν and εἰς τὸν θάνατον αὐτοῦ — both mark the sphere/result into which the baptism places the believer.

Romans 6:16 — "you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness." εἰς θάνατον and εἰς δικαιοσύνην — both mark result: each pattern of obedience leads to (results in) its respective outcome.

Romans 6:19 — "so now present your members as slaves to righteousness leading to sanctification." εἰς ἁγιασμόν — marks result: righteousness, when consistently presented, results in sanctification.

Romans 8:15 — "for the purpose of fear." εἰς φόβον, an accusative of purpose.

Romans 8:28 — "for the purpose of good." εἰς ἀγαθόν — the preposition with the accusative expressing purpose or result: not merely a favorable outcome but the entire designed result of the divine plan.

ὑπέρ — Substitution

Expresses substitution: "instead of," "in place of."

Romans 5:6 — "Christ died for the ungodly." ὑπὲρ ἀσεβῶν — substitution: Christ died in the place of the ungodly, not merely on their behalf in a general sense.

Romans 5:7 — "For one will scarcely die for a righteous person." ὑπὲρ δικαίου — the same substitutionary preposition applied to the human analogy Paul introduces.

Romans 5:8 — "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν — substitution: Christ's death stands in the place of what the sinner would otherwise owe.

Romans 8:32 — "gave him up for us all." ὑπέρ plus the genitive of πᾶς and the first-person pronoun expresses substitutionary atonement: on behalf of, in place of, all of us.

Romans 8:26 — the compound ὑπερεντυγχάνει (see the vocabulary entry, section XIX) is itself built on ὑπέρ, "on behalf of," fused directly into the verb.

ἀπό — "From," Marking Separation or Source

Romans 7:9 — "I was once alive apart from the law." χωρὶς νόμου (using χωρίς rather than ἀπό, an improper preposition meaning "apart from, separate from") marks Paul's condition as one of separation from the law's diagnostic function specifically — not physical distance but conceptual/experiential separation from the law's exposing work.

ἐν — Locative of Sphere, and the Formula "In Christ"

The preposition ἐν with the locative marks either a literal location or, throughout Paul, the sphere of positional union with Christ — the standard formula for positional truth.

Romans 3:24 — "and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ (ἐν + locative/dative) marks the sphere in which redemption is located — positional truth language consistent with Paul's usage throughout Romans.

Romans 8:1 — "no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ is the standard positional-truth formula, marking the sphere of the believer's union with Christ established by the baptism of the Holy Spirit.

Romans 8:2 — "in Christ Jesus" recurs in the same technical sense.

Romans 8:9 — "you are... in the Spirit." ἐν πνεύματι, locative of sphere, marking the believer's realm of existence as under the Spirit's domain rather than the flesh's.

XVIII.Particles and Conjunctions

Romans 2:3 — "Do you suppose... that you will escape the judgment of God?" ὅτι here is objective, introducing the content of what is presumed rather than functioning causally: "that you will escape" — the object of the self-righteous person's presumption.

Romans 3:9 — "What then? Are we Jews any better off? Not at all." The abrupt volley of short rhetorical questions is Paul's own debater's technique, immediately overturning the very premise he has voiced.

γάρ — Inferential/Explanatory "For"

Introduces the ground or explanation of a preceding statement.

Romans 1:18 — "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven." γάρ here is illative — "therefore, for this reason" — the logical linchpin connecting the revelation of God's justice in blessing (v. 17) to its revelation in judgment (v. 18): because justice is revealed as blessing for the adjusted, it is therefore also revealed as judgment against the maladjusted.

Romans 2:1 — "For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself." γάρ here is explanatory and causal: it introduces the reason the self-righteous judge stands without defense — in the very sphere in which he judges others, he practices the same things.

Romans 2:25 — "For circumcision indeed is of value if you obey the law." γάρ gives the apodosis its explanatory, confirmatory force: "so on the one hand, circumcision is indeed beneficial."

Romans 3:2 — "To begin with, the Jews were entrusted with the oracles of God." πρῶτον μέν does not mean "chiefly," as older translations render it, but "in the first place" — the primary item in an intended sequence of advantages, completed by γάρ and ὅτι: "for since in the first place."

Romans 3:3 — "Well then, how stands the case with regard to the two alternatives?" τί γάρ, an idiom composed of the interrogative τί and the postpositive γάρ, is the Koine equivalent of the classical τί πότερος: it introduces two mutually exclusive alternatives — here, adjustment and maladjustment to the justice of God — not a literal "for what?"

Romans 3:22–23 — "for there is no distinction: for all have sinned." γάρ in its explanatory function occurs twice in close succession, each time supplying the grounds for the preceding assertion: no distinction between Jew and Gentile, because all alike have sinned.

Romans 3:28 — "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." γάρ here draws the concluding inference of the entire argument developed since 3:21.

Romans 4:2 — "For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about." γάρ introduces the ground for the rhetorical question of verse 1.

Romans 4:3 — "For what does the Scripture say?" γάρ introduces the scriptural warrant.

Romans 4:13 — "For the promise to Abraham... did not come through the law." γάρ grounds the preceding assertion about the nature of Abraham's heirship.

Romans 5:6 — "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly." γάρ introduces the explanatory ground of the hope just described.

Romans 5:7 — "For one will scarcely die for a righteous person." γάρ introduces the illustrative human analogy.

Romans 6:7 — "For one who has died has been set free from sin." γάρ grounds the preceding exhortation.

Romans 7:1 — "Do you not know, brothers... that the law is binding on a person only as long as he lives?" γάρ (implicit in the surrounding argument's structure) introduces the illustrative principle that follows.

Romans 7:18 — "For I know that nothing good dwells in me." γάρ grounds Paul's preceding admission with a stated reason.

Romans 8:2 — "For the law of the Spirit of life has set you free." γάρ grounds the "no condemnation" declaration of verse 1 with its causal explanation.

Romans 8:3 — "For God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do." γάρ explains why the law's righteous requirement needed a different solution.

Romans 8:15 — "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery." γάρ grounds the assertion of verse 14 (that those led by the Spirit are sons of God).

Romans 8:16 — introduces the following clause's tie to what precedes: the Spirit's witness explains and confirms the sonship just asserted.

Romans 8:38 — "For I am sure that neither death nor life..." γάρ grounds Paul's confidence in the preceding rhetorical question-and-answer sequence about separation from Christ's love.

οὖν — Inferential "Therefore"

Marks a logical conclusion drawn from what precedes.

Romans 2:1 — "Therefore you have no excuse, O man." διό is an inferential conjunction introducing a self-evidently drawn conclusion — distinguished from οὖν, which signals a non-obvious inference requiring deliberate reasoning: the verdict on self-righteousness follows necessarily and immediately from chapter 1.

Romans 3:1 — "Then what advantage has the Jew?" οὖν is inferential, signaling that the question arises from the demonstration in chapter 2 that divine justice is perfectly impartial toward Jew and Gentile alike.

Romans 3:31 — "Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law." ἀλλά here is the strong adversative introducing the positive counter-assertion after the categorical negation μὴ γένοιτο — "on the contrary," not the weaker "yea" of older translations. οὖν, opening the verse, draws the inference from the unity of the Godhead established in verse 30.

Romans 5:1 — "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God." οὖν draws the conclusion of the entire argument of chapters 1–4.

Romans 6:1 — "What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin?" οὖν ties the rhetorical question to the preceding discussion of grace abounding.

Romans 6:12 — "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body." οὖν draws the practical imperative from the doctrinal argument just completed.

Romans 7:3 — "Accordingly, she will be called an adulteress if she lives with another man while her husband is alive." οὖν (implicit as "accordingly" in the sense-flow) draws the conclusion from the principle stated in verse 2.

Romans 7:25 — "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind." ἄρα οὖν, a doubled inferential construction, drawing the strongest possible logical conclusion from the entire preceding wretched-man argument.

Romans 8:1 — "There is therefore now no condemnation." ἄρα (a close cousin of οὖν, also inferential) draws the triumphant conclusion of the whole argument running from Romans 5 through 7.

Romans 8:12 — "So then, brothers, we are debtors, not to the flesh." ἄρα οὖν again, drawing the practical obligation from the doctrinal argument of the preceding verses.

Romans 8:31 — "What then shall we say to these things?" οὖν ties the triumphant rhetorical question to the entire preceding chain of doctrine.

διό — "Therefore, For Which Reason"

A stronger and more formal inferential particle than οὖν, typically introducing a more weighty or climactic conclusion.

Romans 4:22 — "That is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness." διό introduces the climactic conclusion of the entire Abraham illustration.

hoti — Multiple Functions: Causal, Recitative (Quotation), Substantive ("That"), Epexegetical

A single Greek word covering several distinct grammatical functions that English must render with different constructions.

Romans 4:3 — "For what does the Scripture say? 'Abraham believed God...'" ὅτι here is recitative, introducing a direct quotation (equivalent to opening quotation marks) rather than being translated as a word at all.

Romans 4:17 (introducing the Genesis 17:5 quotation) — ὅτι again functions recitatively.

Romans 5:3 — "knowing that suffering produces endurance." ὅτι here introduces a substantive clause functioning as the object of "knowing" — the "that" of indirect statement.

Romans 6:9 — "We know that Christ, being raised from the dead, will never die again." ὅτι as substantive/object clause after εἰδότες.

Romans 7:14 — "For we know that the law is spiritual." ὅτι as substantive/object clause.

Romans 8:16 — "the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God." ὅτι as substantive clause, the content of the joint testimony.

Romans 8:38–39's closing "For I am sure that neither death nor life..." — ὅτι here is epexegetical, unpacking the specific content of Paul's settled conviction (πέπεισμαι) named at the head of the sentence — functioning almost as a colon in English, introducing the itemized list that follows rather than a simple "that"-clause.

Disjunctive ἤ — "Or"

Romans 4:9 — "Is this blessing then only for the circumcised, or also for the uncircumcised?" ἤ poses the genuine alternative the argument will go on to resolve.

Romans 6:16 — "either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness." ἤ structures the two mutually exclusive options.

καθώς / καθάπερ καί — "Just As," "Even As"

Romans 4:6 — "just as David also speaks of the blessing." καθάπερ introduces the supporting scriptural parallel.

Romans 1:28 — "And since they did not see fit to acknowledge God." καθώς (paired with καί: καὶ καθώς) functions as a causal conjunction, making the verse a logical consequence of the entire sequence of verses 18–27: and since all of this — since they rejected after testing — therefore the third judicial "delivery" follows.

Romans 5:12 — "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man..." ὥσπερ introduces the extended Adam-Christ analogy that structures the remainder of the chapter.

ἀλλά — Strong Adversative "But"

Marks a sharp contrast, stronger than the mild adversative δέ.

Romans 2:13 — "For it is not the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law who will be justified." The ἀλλά here is confirmatory rather than adversative: following a negative clause, it intensifies rather than contrasts — "in fact, not" — carrying the negation forward with even greater force: not only is the first assumption false, the second, seemingly stronger one is equally false.

Romans 3:27 — "Then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded. By what kind of law? Of works? No, but by the law of faith." The contrast turns on οὐχί... ἀλλά, decisively excluding the law of works and asserting the law of faith in its place.

Romans 4:16 — "That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace." The surrounding argument's contrast between law and grace is regularly marked by ἀλλά at the sharpest turns.

Romans 5:3 — "we rejoice in our sufferings" — the sharp turn from the expected (suffering as purely negative) to the actual apostolic response is of the kind ἀλλά typically marks.

Romans 6:14 — "since you are not under law but under grace." ἀλλά marks the sharp, categorical contrast between the two governing systems.

Romans 7:19 — "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing." ἀλλά marks the stark contrast Paul draws between intention and practice.

Romans 8:9 — "You, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit." ἀλλά marks the categorical contrast (see also the Proleptic Word Order entry, section XIV, on the emphatic σύ that precedes it).

Romans 8:32 — "he did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all." ἀλλά marks the contrast between what God did not do and what he did do.

Romans 8:37 — "No, in all these things we are more than conquerors." ἀλλά opens the verse with the sharpest possible contrast to the preceding catalogue of hardships.

δέ — Mild Adversative or Simple Connective "But/And"

Weaker than ἀλλά; often merely continuative rather than sharply adversative. Occasionally used for emphatic, almost intensive effect when combined with a proleptic pronoun (see section XIV).

Romans 2:2 — "We know that the judgment of God rightly falls." δέ, at the head of this verse, is adversative, establishing a contrast between the presumptive judgment of the self-righteous person in verse 1 and the perfect judicial verdict of God in verse 2.

Romans 2:17 — "But if you call yourself a Jew." δέ functions adversatively, contrasting the self-righteous Gentile of verses 1–16 with the self-righteous Jew of verses 17–29.

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind, a light to those who are in darkness." δέ functions additively here rather than adversatively — better rendered "moreover" — indicating a close, progressive relationship between the self-descriptions of verses 17–18 and the further claims of verse 19.

Romans 4:5 — "and to the one who does not work but believes." δέ provides the mild connective contrast between the two categories of person.

Romans 5:8 — "but God shows his love for us." δέ connects this verse's demonstration back to the human analogy of verse 7, with a contrast of degree rather than outright opposition.

Romans 8:9 — "You, however, are not in the flesh" — the same δέ discussed above under ἀλλά functions here in its emphatic-intensive capacity (paired with the proleptic σύ) rather than as a plain adversative; Greek allows δέ this heightened, "in fact/indeed" force in certain emphatic constructions.

Romans 8:26 — "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness." δέ, here mildly connective, ties the Spirit's help to the wider context of hope and suffering just discussed.

Standalone Emphatic μέν

Used alone (without a following δέ in the same breath) to intensify or affirm a statement — "as a matter of fact," "indeed, in fact."

Romans 7:12 — "So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good." The μέν here (rendered "so" in most English translations, which under-represents its force) functions as a strong affirming particle: "as a matter of fact, the law is holy" — a firm, settled assertion closing off any doubt raised by the preceding verses.

ἵνα — Purpose Clauses

Introduces a subjunctive purpose clause, "in order that."

Romans 4:16 — "in order that the promise may rest on grace." ἵνα introduces the purpose of the arrangement.

Romans 5:21 — "so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign." ἵνα introduces the purpose/result of the whole preceding contrast between Adam and Christ.

Romans 6:4 — "in order that... we too might walk in newness of life." ἵνα introduces the purpose of the baptism-into-death imagery.

Romans 6:6 — "so that the body of sin might be brought to nothing." ἵνα introduces the purpose of the co-crucifixion.

Romans 7:4 — "so that we may bear fruit for God." ἵνα introduces the purpose of the believer's death to the law through Christ's body.

Romans 8:4 — "in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us." ἵνα introduces the purpose clause discussed at length under the Culminative Aorist and Potential Subjunctive entries above.

Romans 8:17 — "provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him." ἵνα introduces the purpose of the fellowship of suffering.

ὥστε — Inferential "So That," "Therefore"

Introduces a result or inferential clause, related to but distinct from purpose clauses with ἵνα.

Romans 7:4 — "Likewise, my brothers, you also have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another." ὥστε introduces the resulting state that flows from the believer's death to the law.

Romans 7:12 — "So the law is holy" — while the dominant particle here is the emphatic μέν discussed above, the inferential weight of the whole sentence functions in the same conclusion-drawing register as ὥστε elsewhere in Paul.

XIX.Vocabulary Distinguishing Related Greek Terms

δοῦλος — "Slave," Not "Servant"

The KJV's "servant" is too dignified a rendering and misses the doctrinal point entirely.

Romans 1:1 — "Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus." δοῦλος means slave, without qualification. Under a perfect master, all rights belong to the master; the slave has only privileges — blessing through adjustment to the justice of God, discipline through maladjustment. Slavery under a perfect master is the ideal security status, not a diminishment.

ἀπόστολος — Etymology from Attic Naval Command

Romans 1:1 — "called to be an apostle." ἀπόστολος derives from Attic Greek naval terminology: when Athens sent its fleet into engagement, the council elevated one admiral above all others as supreme commander — the ἀπόστολος, with absolute authority for the duration of the mission. In the New Testament this becomes the highest spiritual gift given the Church: never simply "one sent" or "missionary," but supreme, temporary authority during the pre-canon period, closed permanently with the completion of the canon.

εὐαγγέλιον — Broader Than "Salvation Message"

Romans 1:1, 16 — "set apart for the gospel of God"; "I am not ashamed of the gospel." εὐαγγέλιον encompasses the entire realm of doctrine that constitutes encouraging news for the believer, not only the message that saves the unbeliever — the grace plan, logistical provision, rebound, supergrace, and every blessing the justice of God provides through the maturity adjustment. Narrowing the word to the salvation message alone is a persistent exegetical error.

δύναμις θεοῦ — Ability, Not Raw Power

Romans 1:16 — "for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes." δύναμις traces to an Indo-European root meaning ability, capacity for intellectual and spiritual function — competence, not brute force. The gospel is the ability of God: it reveals and activates the divine competence freed by the cross to act on behalf of anyone who believes.

ἐκ πίστεως εἰς πίστιν — From Faith to Doctrine

Romans 1:17 — "from faith for faith." ἐκ πίστεως (ablative case) uses πίστις in its active sense — the once-for-all act of trusting Christ that begins phase two. εἰς πίστιν (accusative case) shifts πίστις to its passive sense — the body of doctrine progressively assimilated through the daily function of GAP. The whole phrase traces the complete arc of the believer's temporal life: from the non-meritorious act of faith at salvation to the ongoing body of doctrine that produces maturity.

ὀργή θεοῦ — Judicial, Not Emotional, Anger

Romans 1:18 — "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven." ὀργή is an Attic word consistently connoting moral, judicial anger — principled indignation that protects from evil, not God "losing his temper." Demosthenes coined the phrase δικαία ὀργή, "just anger." The Roman equivalent, ira Deum, was used by Cicero for the fall of Corinth and Carthage and by Tacitus for Nero's reign of terror — a phrase Paul's Roman readers, writing under Nero, would have recognized immediately.

ἀσέβεια vs. ἀδικία — Heathenism and Anti-Justice

Romans 1:18 — "against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men." ἀσέβεια (alpha-privative + σέβομαι, "to worship, hold in awe") is non-godliness — refusal to acknowledge God at the point of God-consciousness or gospel-hearing. ἀδικία (alpha-privative + δίκη/δίκαιος) is the precise negation of the justice-word family: where δικαιοσύνη is the justice of God, ἀδικία is anti-justice, the condition of being maladjusted to that judicial order. Together the two terms cover the full spectrum of salvation maladjustment that the rest of the chapter describes.

κατέχω — Habitual Suppression, Not a Single Act

Romans 1:18 — "who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth." κατέχω (κατά + ἔχω, "to hold down, hold back") in the customary present describes the characteristic, ongoing behavior of the person in maladjustment — a sustained pattern of life, not a one-time rejection; each act of suppression deepens the pattern and accelerates the darkening described in verses 21–22.

ἀναπολόγητος — Without Legal Defense

Romans 1:20 — "so that they are without excuse." ἀναπολόγητος (alpha-privative + ἀπολογέομαι, "to make a legal defense before a court") means, precisely, without defense before a court of justice — not "without apology" in the modern sense. No member of the human race with normal mentality has any excuse, since God-consciousness has been available since creation.

ματαιόω / ματαιότης — The Vacuum of the Soul

Romans 1:21 — "they became futile in their thinking." ματαιόω means to make empty, to fill with worthlessness; the cognate noun ματαιότης names the vacuum itself — the emptiness that opens in the soul after negative volition, into which evil thinking rushes. Not the KJV's "became vain in their imaginations" but "received worthless thoughts."

ἀσύνετος — Deliberate, Not Neutral, Non-Comprehension

Romans 1:21 — "their foolish hearts were darkened" (more precisely, their non-understanding right lobe). ἀσύνετος (alpha-privative + σύνετος, "understanding") always carries a critical connotation — never merely neutral ignorance. It encompasses deliberate ignorance: the non-use of the mind, the refusal to think, the spiritual laziness that lets the soul remain in darkness.

μωραίνω — "Fool," Sharing a Root with "Moron"

Romans 1:22 — "Claiming to be wise, they became fools." μωραίνω traces through Attic μωρός (fool) to the English "moron" and the Latin morus — one of the widest Indo-European root distributions in the language family. To become a μωρός is also to become tasteless, insipid, saltless — used of both people and food.

παρά + accusative — Exclusive, Not Comparative, Exchange

Romans 1:25 — "who worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator." παρά with the accusative denotes mutually exclusive comparison — "rather than," never "more than." The creature is chosen to the total exclusion of the Creator, not merely preferred above him.

κατεργάζομαι — Working Out What Is Already Within

Romans 1:27 — "committing shameless acts... and receiving in themselves the due penalty." κατεργάζομαι (κατά, intensive + ἐργάζομαι, "to work") means to work out what is already on the inside — to accomplish, produce, execute what is inwardly motivated. The same verb recurs at Romans 2:9 for the general production of evil from within, and later in Romans 7 in a related context of inner conflict.

δοκιμάζω / ἀδόκιμος — A Deliberate Paranomasia

Romans 1:28 — "And since they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them up to a debased mind." δοκιμάζω means to test, examine, and approve — or, negated, to reject after testing. The unbeliever tested (ἐδοκίμασαν, negated) God and said no; God tests (same root) him in turn and delivers a disqualified (ἀδόκιμος) mind — a deliberate wordplay linking the human act of rejection to its divine consequence.

πληρόω — Intensive Perfect of Total Saturation

Romans 1:29 — "They were filled with all manner of unrighteousness." πεπληρωμένους (perfect passive participle of πληρόω) is an intensive perfect: a permanently established state of saturation with ongoing repercussions, not a momentary filling. There is no exact English equivalent — closest is "fully possessed" or "saturated."

κακία — The Practice, Not Merely the State, of Evil

Romans 1:29 — "envy, murder, strife, deceit, maliciousness." κακία names the outworking or actual practice of evil — distinct from πονηρία (the state of evil) and πλεονεξία (the desire for evil). It is evil in its external manifestation, and the entire catalogue that follows in verses 29b–31 specifies its content.

ἐπαναπαύομαι — Leaning One's Full Weight

Romans 2:17 — "But if you call yourself a Jew and rely on the law." ἐπαναπαύω (ἐπί + ἀναπαύω, "to rest, cease from labor") means to lean one's entire weight upon something. The present middle indicative — a dynamic middle — depicts the legalist's total, self-generated dependence on the law as an instrument of salvation it was never designed to bear.

ἐποναμάζω vs. ὀνομάζω — A Deliberate Compound Choice

Romans 2:17 — Paul's choice of the compound ἐπονομάζω ("to classify, to surname") over the simple ὀνομάζω ("to name") is exegetically significant: the simple verb would have affirmed Jewish identity unreservedly, while the compound redirects attention to classification and category — a hapax legomenon in the New Testament.

καυχάομαι — Habitual, Self-Promoting Boast

Romans 2:23 — "You who boast in the law dishonor God by breaking the law." καυχάομαι, a deponent verb (middle in form, active in meaning), appears here in the customary present: the habitual, ongoing boast of the legalist who turns the law into a vehicle for self-promotion rather than an instrument of universal condemnation.

δικαίωμα — Legal Requirement, Not Mere Regulation

Romans 1:32; 2:26 — "Though they know God's righteous decree..."; "the righteous requirements of the law." δικαίωμα originally denotes a legal claim or judicial verdict; in these contexts it names the righteous requirement or legal regulation of the justice of God — in 1:32, specifically the requirement to believe in Christ, known fully and rejected; in 2:26, the requirements the law depicts across all three adjustments to God's justice.

γράμμα — Letters Without Meaning

Romans 2:27 — the term γράμμα ("a letter of the alphabet," by extension "written code") is used as sanctified sarcasm: for the Jew who rejects Christ, the Mosaic law is nothing more than Hebrew letters assembled without meaning — no doctrinal content is accessible apart from salvation adjustment.

περιτομή — Ritual Value Only in Its Doctrinal Content

Romans 2:25, 29 — "For circumcision indeed is of value if you obey the law"; "circumcision of the heart." περιτομή (περί + τέμνω, "to cut around") names the founding ritual of the Jewish race, first performed on Abraham. Its anarthrous construction in 2:25 emphasizes its qualitative, doctrinally-grounded character rather than the bare institutional fact — the rite pointed toward maximum adjustment to the justice of God, and had no value divorced from that content. At 2:29, περιτομὴ καρδίας — "circumcision of the heart" — designates maximum doctrine resident in the soul.

συνείδησις — Conscience as Joint Knowledge

Romans 2:15 — "They show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness." συνείδησις (σύν + οἶδα, "to know with") is the exact semantic parent of the English "conscience" (Latin con- + scire): the repository of norms in the right lobe by which conduct is evaluated, functioning in the Gentile without the Mosaic law just as it functions in the Jew who has studied it.

κρίμα vs. κρίνω vs. κατακρίνω — A Family of Judgment Terms

Romans 2:1–2 — "you condemn yourself... the judgment of God rightly falls." κρίνω is the primary verb "to judge," used of both God's official judicial verdict and, when a self-righteous human is the subject, of personal maligning or slander. κατακρίνω (κατά + κρίνω) intensifies this into self-condemnation. κρίμα, the noun, names the full judgment-action of God specifically, distinguished from every human attempt at judgment.

ὀνόμα — Full Personal Identity and Reputation

Romans 1:5 — "for the sake of his name." ὄνομα encompasses far more than a bare label — the full personal identity and reputation of Christ — making the purpose clause of the verse a statement about glorifying the whole of who Christ is, not a formula invoked mechanically.

ἀπολύτρωσις — Ransom-Purchased Deliverance

Romans 3:24 — "through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." ἀπολύτρωσις (ἀπό + λύτρον, "ransom") names deliverance procured specifically by the payment of a ransom — used in both secular and biblical Greek for the liberation of slaves or captives through a purchase price, not deliverance in some vaguer, unpriced sense.

ἱλαστήριον — The Mercy Seat, Publicly Displayed

Romans 3:25 — "whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood." ἱλαστήριον is the Septuagint's term for the golden mercy seat atop the Ark of the Covenant, flanked by two cherubim representing God's righteousness and justice; the blood applied there on the Day of Atonement is the type of which Christ, publicly displayed, is declared the antitype and fulfillment — the place where divine justice is publicly and permanently satisfied.

πάρεσις vs. ἄφεσις — Suspension, Not Remission

Romans 3:25 — "in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins." πάρεσις is carefully distinct from ἄφεσις ("forgiveness/remission"): it denotes a judicial holding-back of judgment rather than a permanent settlement of it — the suspension of judgment on pre-cross sins, pending their definitive judgment in Christ at the cross.

κραυχῆσις — The Entire Complex of Self-Righteous Claim

Romans 3:27 — "Then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded." καύχησις (from καυχάομαι) names not a single boastful remark but the entire complex of arrogance, self-righteousness, and claimed human merit before God — excluded categorically by both divine integrity and the principle of faith.

νόμος — Two Senses Within a Single Argument

Romans 3:27, 31 — "By what kind of law? Of works? No, but by the law of faith"; "Do we then overthrow the law?" In 3:27, νόμος shifts from the Mosaic Law proper to the broader sense of a governing principle — contrasting the principle of works (νόμος ἔργων) with the principle of faith (νόμος πίστεως). In 3:31, νόμος returns to its ordinary sense, the Mosaic law itself, whose true purpose — condemnation pointing to Christ — faith establishes rather than abrogates.

παράπτωμα vs. ἁμαρτία — "Trespass" vs. "Sin"

παράπτωμα denotes a specific act, a falling-aside from a known standard; ἁμαρτία denotes the underlying sin nature or the general principle of sin, often personified.

Romans 5:15–20 uses παράπτωμα consistently for Adam's single, specific act of disobedience (the trespass), while ἁμαρτία in the same stretch of argument (verse 12, verse 20, verse 21) denotes sin as a ruling principle or power that entered the world and reigns.

Romans 4:7 — "blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered." Both ἀνομίαι ("lawless deeds") and ἁμαρτίαι appear here as near-synonyms for specific acts, distinct from the more technical, principial use ἁμαρτία carries later in chapters 5–7.

Romans 5:12 — "sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin" — ἁμαρτία personified as an invasive power, not a specific deed.

Romans 7:7–11 — ἁμαρτία is consistently personified as an aggressive agent (it "seizes an opportunity," "deceives," "kills") — the sin nature as a ruling entity, not a catalogue of specific deeds.

χάρισμα / δωρεά / δώρημα — Three Words for "Gift"

Each carries a distinct nuance: χάρισμα stresses the grace-basis of the gift; δωρεά stresses its free, unearned character; δώρημα stresses the gift as a completed, presented object.

Romans 5:15 — "the free gift" translates δώρημα in this specific occurrence (referring to the entirety of God's saving provision, presented as a completed object), while later in the same verse "the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man" shifts to χάρισμα, stressing that the gift flows specifically from grace as its source.

Romans 5:16 — "the free gift" here is δώρημα again, presenting the whole saving provision as an object already given, in contrast to the singular "judgment following one trespass."

Romans 5:17 — "the free gift of righteousness" is δωρεά, stressing the absolutely unearned, uncompensated character of the righteousness imputed.

Romans 6:23 — "the free gift of God is eternal life" is χάρισμα, stressing that eternal life flows specifically from grace, standing in deliberate contrast to "wages" (ὀψώνια, see below), which are earned.

κατάκριμα vs. δικαίωμα — "Condemnation" vs. "Justification/Righteous Act"

κατάκριμα denotes the judicial sentence of condemnation flowing from a violation; δικαίωμα denotes either a righteous act or the legal verdict of righteousness flowing from it.

Romans 5:16 — "the judgment following one trespass brought condemnation" (κατάκριμα), "but the free gift following many trespasses brought justification" (δικαίωμα) — the two nouns are deliberately paired and contrasted within a single verse, each naming the respective judicial outcome of Adam's one trespass versus Christ's provision covering many trespasses.

Romans 5:18 — "one trespass led to condemnation for all men" (κατάκριμα) — "so one act of righteousness [δικαίωμα] leads to justification and life for all men" — the same paired contrast recurs, closing the inclusio begun at verse 16.

Romans 8:1 — "There is therefore now no condemnation [κατάκρίμα] for those who are in Christ Jesus." The same noun from Romans 5:16 and 18 recurs here as the capstone of the entire argument: the κατάκριμα inherited from Adam has been fully and finally answered.

ὑπακοή vs. παρακοή — "Obedience" vs. "Disobedience" (a wordplay on the same root)

Both share the root ἀκούω ("to hear"); ὑπακοή is literally "hearing under [authority]," while παρακοή is "hearing amiss, mishearing."

Romans 5:19 — "For as by the one man's disobedience [παρακοή] the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience [ὑπακοή] the many will be made righteous." Paul deliberately exploits the shared root of these two nouns to sharpen the Adam-Christ parallel: both acts are, at root, a matter of hearing (or mishearing) the spoken word of God.

βασιλεύω vs. κυριεύω — "To Reign" vs. "To Be Lord/Master Over"

βασιλεύω denotes reigning as a king over a realm; κυριεύω denotes exercising the authority of a lord or master over a subject.

Romans 5:14, 17, 21 use βασιλεύω consistently for the reign of death and the reign of grace — kingdom-scale, realm-wide dominion.

Romans 6:9, 14; 7:1 use κυριεύω for more personal, direct lordship — death's mastery over Christ (denied), sin's mastery over the believer (negated by grace), and the law's mastery over a person (limited to the duration of life).

ὀψώνια vs. χάρισμα — "Wages" vs. "Free Gift"

ὀψώνια denotes a soldier's ration or pay, earned by service; χάρισμα denotes an unearned grace-gift (see above).

Romans 6:23 — "For the wages [ὀψώνια] of sin is death, but the free gift [χάρισμα] of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." The military metaphor of ὀψώνια is deliberate: sin pays its soldiers exactly what they have earned — death — while God's provision is not a wage at all but an unearned gift.

ἀνήρ vs. ἄνθρωπος — "Husband/Male" vs. "Human Being"

ἀνήρ specifies a male person, often in the specific sense of "husband"; ἄνθρωπος denotes a human being generically, without reference to sex.

Romans 5:12 — "sin came into the world through one man [ἄνθρωπος]" — Adam here is named generically as a human being, the federal head of the human race as a whole, not specifically in his role as husband.

Romans 7:2–3 uses ἀνήρ consistently and specifically for "husband," the legally binding marriage partner — a different word entirely from the ἄνθρωπος of Romans 5:12, and the distinction matters: Paul's marriage analogy in chapter 7 depends on ἀνήρ's specifically marital sense, not humanity in general.

χρηματίζω vs. καλέω — Two Words Sometimes Both Rendered "To Call"

χρηματίζω denotes a formal, official, or legal classification/designation; καλέω denotes an invitation, summons, or (in Paul's technical theological usage) the act of divine election.

Romans 7:3 — "she will be called [χρηματίσει] an adulteress" — a legal-classificatory verb (see the Nomic Future entry, section III): this is an official categorization, not an invitation.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he called [ἐκάλεσεν] he also justified" — καλέω here carries its full technical theological weight: God's effectual summons of the elect, an entirely different semantic domain from χρηματίζω's legal classification.

ἀναζάω — "To Come Alive Again," "To Spring to Life"

A compound of ἀνά ("again, up") plus ζάω ("to live") — describing a dormant condition suddenly activated, not an initial coming-into-being.

Romans 7:9 — "sin... sprang to life [ἀνέζησεν]." The sin nature did not come into existence when the commandment arrived — it was already present from birth — but it "sprang to life" in the specific sense of becoming active, aggressive, and consciously felt, the moment the commandment gave it something to react against.

οἰκέω / κατοικέω — "To Dwell / To Dwell Permanently"

οἰκέω denotes ordinary dwelling or residence; the compound κατοικέω (not used in this stretch of Romans, but relevant by contrast) intensifies the sense toward permanent settlement.

Romans 7:17, 20 — "sin that dwells [οἰκοῦσα] within me" — the simple verb οἰκέω is used, not the intensive compound, yet the retroactive progressive present tense (see section III) itself supplies the force of permanence: the sin nature's residence, though named with the simpler verb, is nonetheless total and unbroken.

Romans 8:9 — "the Spirit of God dwells [οἰκεῖ] in you" — the same simple verb, now describing the Holy Spirit's indwelling — a deliberate parallel: both the sin nature and the Holy Spirit are said to "dwell" using the identical verb, setting up the direct contest between the two indwelling powers that structures the whole of Romans 8.

ῥύομαι — "To Rescue by Dragging to Safety"

A verb specifically depicting rescue of someone who cannot move himself — dragging a victim out of danger, not merely offering assistance.

Romans 7:24 — "Who will deliver [ῥύσεται] me from this body of death?" (see the Progressive Future entry, section V) — the verb itself establishes total helplessness on Paul's part: this is not a request for aid to someone who could otherwise struggle free, but an admission that only an external agent, dragging him bodily to safety, can resolve his condition.

κατάκριμα vs. κατακρίνω — Noun and Verb of the Same Root

κατάκριμα is the noun (the sentence of condemnation itself); κατακρίνω is the verb (the act of pronouncing or executing that condemnation).

Romans 8:1 — "no condemnation [κατάκριμα]" — the noun, naming the standing judicial sentence now lifted.

Romans 8:3 — "he condemned [κατέκρινεν] sin in the flesh" — the verb, naming the specific act by which that sentence was executed against sin itself (not against the believer) at the cross.

τέκνον vs. υἱός — "Child" vs. "Son"

τέκνον stresses the fact of birth/origin; υἱός stresses legal standing, maturity, and the full privileges of the relationship — a distinction Paul exploits deliberately across Romans 8.

Romans 8:16 — "the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children [τέκνα] of God" — τέκνον here stresses the fact of the new birth: every believer, without exception, is a τέκνον the instant he believes.

Romans 8:14 — "For all who are led by the Spirit of God are sons [υἱοί] of God" — υἱός here stresses the legal standing and maturity implied by the term: not every τέκνον is functioning, experientially, as a υἱός; sonship in this fuller sense correlates with being led by the Spirit — a matter of function, not mere birth-status.

Romans 8:19 — "the revealing of the sons [υἱῶν] of God" — again υἱός, the mature, functioning status that the created order awaits the disclosure of.

Romans 8:21 — "the glorious liberty of the children [τέκνων] of God" — here τέκνον recurs, in a context stressing the birth-relationship shared by all believers rather than the mature-functioning distinction of υἱός.

ζωή — "Life"

Used across Romans 8 in several distinguishable senses, always the specific quality of eternal, uncreated, divine life rather than mere biological existence (βίος).

Romans 8:2 — "the law of the Spirit of life" — ζωή as the animating principle the Holy Spirit supplies and represents (see the Ablative of Source entry, section XVI).

Romans 8:6 — "to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace" — ζωή as the present experiential quality of the Spirit-filled life, contrasted with the "death" (θάνατος) that characterizes the mind set on the flesh.

Romans 8:10 — "the Spirit is life because of righteousness" — ζωή as a present possession flowing directly from imputed righteousness.

Romans 8:11 — "he... will also give life to your mortal bodies" — ζωή in its future, physical-resurrection sense.

πληρόω — Four Distinguishable Senses Across the Guide

The verb "to fill, to fulfill, to complete" carries at least four distinguishable senses depending on context (see also the Culminative Aorist entry at Romans 8:4, section I).

To fill a space or container (not directly exemplified in this passage, but the base sense underlying the metaphorical extensions below).

To fulfill a requirement or obligation — Romans 8:4, "the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled [πληρωθῇ] in us" — the maturity-adjustment sense, discussed fully under the Culminative Aorist.

To complete or bring to its intended conclusion a process already underway.

To fill emotionally or spiritually, as with confidence or conviction — compare πληροφορηθείς at Romans 4:21, a related but distinct compound (πλήρης + φέρω) meaning "to be fully persuaded, fully carried along to conviction," rather than πληρόω's more general "to fill/fulfill."

ἀδύνατος — "Powerless," "Impossible"

An adjective built from the alpha-privative plus δύναμις ("power, ability"): literally "without power."

Romans 8:3 — "For God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do." The underlying τὸ ἀδύνατον τοῦ νόμου ("the powerlessness of the law," see the Nominative Absolute entry, section XIV) identifies precisely what was lacking: not a defect in the law's content (which remains "holy and righteous and good," per 7:12) but its total absence of enabling power against the flesh.

ὁμοίωμα — "Likeness," "Form"

A noun stressing correspondence of form or appearance, deliberately chosen to avoid asserting (or denying) full identity.

Romans 8:3 — "in the likeness [ὁμοιώματι] of sinful flesh." The term is doctrinally precise: Christ took on true, genuine human flesh — the likeness is not illusory — yet the term also carefully avoids saying Christ took on "sinful flesh" outright, preserving his sinlessness while affirming the full reality of his humanity.

περὶ ἁμαρτίας — "Concerning Sin," Idiom for "Sin Offering"

The prepositional phrase περὶ ἁμαρτίας, while literally "concerning sin," is the standard Septuagint idiom for the technical term "sin offering" (Hebrew ḥatta'th).

Romans 8:3 — "he condemned sin in the flesh" — immediately preceded in the verse by περὶ ἁμαρτίας, "as a sin offering," identifying Christ's mission specifically in sacrificial, Levitical terms, not merely as a general statement about the topic of sin.

φρονέω — "To Set the Mind On," "To Be Minded"

Denotes not a passing thought but a settled mental orientation or disposition — the entire bent of one's thinking.

Romans 8:5 — "those who live according to the flesh set their minds [φρονοῦσιν] on the things of the flesh" — a retroactive progressive present (see section III) describing a total, sustained mental orientation, not an occasional lapse.

Romans 8:6 — "to set the mind [φρόνημα, the related noun] on the flesh is death, but to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace" — the noun form names the resulting mindset itself as the determining factor of the two contrasted outcomes.

δουλεία + πάλιν — "Slavery" Compounded with "Again, Back"

Romans 8:15 — "you did not receive the spirit of slavery [δουλείας] to fall back into fear." The phrase includes πάλιν ("again, back"), signaling regression to a former condition — the language of reversionism, not merely fear in the abstract.

υἱοθεσία — Compound Etymology: "Son-Placing"

A compound noun built from υἱός ("son") and a form of τίθημι ("to place, to set"): literally "the placing of a son," the technical Greco-Roman legal term for formal adoption.

Romans 8:15, 23 — "the Spirit of adoption [υἱοθεσίας]" and "we... groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons." The term draws directly on Roman adoption law and ceremony: a formal, legal, and public act by which a person is given full sonship rights he did not possess by birth — the perfect legal metaphor for the believer's new standing, since it distinguishes the legal act of adoption from the fact of biological birth (τέκνον, above), even though in the believer's case both realities occur at the identical moment of salvation.

κράζω + Abba, ὁ Πατήρ — Bilingual Cry, Roman Adoption-Ceremony Parallel

κράζω denotes an intense, urgent cry, often (as here) spontaneous and emotional; the bilingual formula Abba (Aramaic) followed immediately by ὁ πατήρ (Greek, "the Father") parallels the actual legal formula spoken in Roman adoption ceremonies, in which the newly adopted son would cry out to his new father using both his original and new household's terms of address.

Romans 8:15 — "by [the Spirit] we cry [κράζομεν], 'Abba! Father!'" The bilingual cry is not merely emotional decoration; it directly echoes the known legal-ceremonial language of Roman adoption, reinforcing the technical legal force of υἱοθεσία immediately preceding it.

συμμαρτυρέω — "To Bear Witness Together With"

A compound of σύν ("with, together") plus μαρτυρέω ("to testify, to bear witness"): a joint, corroborating testimony, not a testimony given in isolation.

Romans 8:16 — "the Spirit himself bears witness with [συμμαρτυρεῖ] our spirit that we are children of God." The compound specifically requires two witnesses in agreement: the Holy Spirit's testimony and the believer's own human spirit, corroborating each other — not the Spirit's testimony alone, imposed from outside, but a joint testimony in which the believer's own spirit concurs.

ἀποκαραδοκία — Triple Compound, Apparently Coined by Paul

Built from ἀπό ("from, away") + κάρα ("head") + δοκέω ("to watch, to expect"): literally, "watching with the head thrust forward and away [from other distractions]" — a term of extreme, single-minded, straining expectation, occurring so rarely in extant Greek literature outside Paul that it may be his own coinage or a very rare technical term he adopted.

Romans 8:19 — "For the creation waits with eager longing [ἀποκαραδοκία] for the revealing of the sons of God." The image is of a person craning the neck forward, head thrust out, ignoring every other distraction, straining every capacity of sight toward a single point on the horizon — the entire material creation pictured in this posture of fixed, single-minded expectancy.

ἀπεκδέχομαι — "To Await Eagerly," Compound Intensive

A double compound (ἀπό + ἐκ + δέχομαι, "to receive/wait") intensifying the ordinary sense of waiting into eager, confident anticipation.

Romans 8:19, 23, 25 — "the creation waits with eager longing" (v.19), "we... groan inwardly as we wait eagerly [ἀπεκδεχόμενοι] for adoption as sons" (v.23), "we wait for it with patience" (v.25, same verb). The repeated use of this intensive compound across the passage structurally unifies the creation's waiting, the believer's waiting, and the general posture of hope Paul is describing throughout the chapter.

ὑπερεντυγχάνω + στεναγμοῖς ἀλαλήτοις — Anthropopathism in Prayer

ὑπερεντυγχάνω is a rare double compound (ὑπέρ + ἐν + τυγχάνω, "to meet with, to intercede") meaning "to intercede on behalf of," intensified beyond the simple ἐντυγχάνω (below); στεναγμοῖς ἀλαλήτοις means "groanings that cannot be uttered [in words]." The description is an anthropopathism — language describing God's action in terms of human emotional/physical experience for the reader's comprehension, not a literal claim that the infinite Holy Spirit groans as a human groans.

Romans 8:26 — "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words." The rare compound verb and its striking object together form one of the most vivid anthropopathic descriptions of the Spirit's ministry anywhere in the New Testament: the Spirit's intercession, in the believer's moment of not knowing how to pray, is described in terms borrowed from the most intense human emotional distress — not because the Spirit is limited as humans are, but because this is the only vocabulary available to convey to the reader the intensity and personal engagement of that intercession.

ἐντυγχάνω — The Simple Form, "To Intercede, To Meet With, To Petition"

The base verb behind the compound above, used elsewhere of both the Spirit's and Christ's intercessory ministry.

Romans 8:27 (referring back to the Spirit's intercession) and Romans 8:34 (referring to Christ's) both draw on this verb family: the Spirit intercedes according to the will of God (v.27), and Christ himself likewise "intercedes for us" (v.34, ἐντυγχάνει) — two distinct, simultaneous intercessory ministries, one from the indwelling Spirit and one from the ascended, session-seated Christ.

συνεργέω — "To Work Together With"

A compound of σύν ("with") plus ἐργάζομαι ("to work, to labor"): cooperative, joint labor producing a single unified result.

Romans 8:28 — "all things work together [συνεργεῖ] for good, for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose." The compound itself supplies the theological content often missed in translation: it is not that each individual thing, taken alone, is good, but that the total complex of circumstances cooperates, works jointly, toward a single designed outcome — and, per the Customary Present entry (section III), this is the exclusive, habitual experience of the believer who has advanced to spiritual maturity, not an automatic promise applicable regardless of spiritual condition.

προγινώσκω / προορίζω — Distinguished from Bare Omniscience

προγινώσκω ("to know beforehand") in Paul's technical theological usage denotes not mere prescient awareness of future facts (which would collapse the term into simple omniscience) but a personal, relational foreknowledge — God's eternal, pre-temporal knowing of and relationship to specific individuals; προορίζω ("to mark out beforehand, to predetermine") denotes the further, distinct act of determining a destiny for those already personally known.

Romans 8:29 — "For those whom he foreknew [προέγνω] he also predestined [προώρισεν] to be conformed to the image of his Son." The distinction between the two verbs is the entire logical structure of the golden chain: foreknowledge (a relational, personal knowing) precedes and grounds predestination (a determined destiny) — the sequence would collapse into meaninglessness if προγινώσκω meant nothing more than God's generic, universal knowledge of all future facts, since that would apply equally to the saved and the lost and could not function as the distinguishing first link of a chain describing only some.

σύμμορφος — "Conformed To, Having the Same Form As"

A compound of σύν ("with, together") plus μορφή ("form, essential nature"): sharing the identical essential form, not merely an external or superficial resemblance.

Romans 8:29 — "to be conformed [σύμμορφος, as the underlying adjective] to the image of his Son" — the term specifies conformity to Christ's essential nature (ultimately, the resurrection body and full experiential righteousness), not merely imitation of his external conduct.

καλέω — Technical Election Sense

See also the χρηματίζω vs. καλέω entry above; the verb, when used of God's action toward believers, is not a general invitation extended to all but the effectual, particular summons of those God has already foreknown and predestined.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he predestined he also called [ἐκάλεσεν], and those whom he called he also justified" — καλέω in this technical sense names the third of the five steps in the golden chain, the point at which God's eternal decree intersects human history in the effectual gospel call.

δικαιόω / δοξάζω — The Golden Chain's Closing Pair

δικαιόω ("to justify," to declare righteous) and δοξάζω ("to glorify") close the five-step chain of Romans 8:29–30, the fourth and fifth links respectively.

Romans 8:30 — "those whom he justified [ἐδικαίωσεν] he also glorified [ἐδόξασεν]." Both verbs are aorist (see the Constative and Culminative Aorist entries, section I): justification names the judicial imputation of righteousness at the moment of faith, and glorification — stated here in the aorist despite being, experientially, still future for every living believer — names the final, guaranteed state of resurrection glory, presented as though already accomplished because it is already fixed in the unbreakable divine decree.

ἴδιος — "One's Own," Emphatic Possession

An adjective stressing personal, exclusive ownership, stronger than the simple possessive pronoun.

Romans 8:32 — "He who did not spare his own [ἰδίου] Son" — the term stresses the uniquely personal, exclusive relationship between the Father and the Son, heightening the magnitude of the sacrifice: this was not simply "a" son given up, but the Father's own, uniquely-possessed Son.

παραδίδωμι — "To Hand Over, To Deliver Up"

A verb of formal, judicial transfer — handing a person over into the custody or power of another, frequently in Paul with judicial or sacrificial overtones.

Romans 8:32 — "gave him up [παρέδωκεν] for us all" — the same verb used at Romans 4:25 of Christ being "delivered up for our trespasses" (see the Divine Passive entry, section VII), here in the active voice with God the Father as the explicit, active subject: the Father himself is the one who hands the Son over to judicial death.

χαρίζομαι — "To Grant Graciously," Built on the Same Root as χάρις

A verb denoting the free, gracious bestowal of something — cognate with χάρις ("grace") and χάρισμα (see above).

Romans 8:32 — "how will he not also graciously give [χαρίσεται] us all things?" (see the Nomic/Deliberative Future entry, section III/V) — the verb's built-in grace-content reinforces the a fortiori logic of the verse: if the far greater gift (the Son) was given up, the lesser gifts that follow are, by the very nature of the giving verb chosen, gracious rather than earned.

ἐγκαλέω — "To Bring a Charge Against," Technical Legal/Judicial Term

A compound of ἐν ("in, against") plus καλέω ("to call"): to call someone into court, to institute formal legal proceedings against them.

Romans 8:33 — "Who shall bring any charge [ἐγκαλέσει] against God's elect?" (see the Deliberative Future entry, section V) — the term is specifically forensic: Paul frames the entire question as a courtroom scene, and the verb itself supplies the legal setting the English translation "bring a charge" only approximates.

ἐκλεκτός — "Elect, Chosen"

The adjective naming those who are the object of God's choosing — directly tied to the doctrine of election developed across the golden chain of verses 29–30.

Romans 8:33 — "God's elect [ἐκλεκτῶν]" — the term functions here as the capstone identification of those protected from any successful accusation: their status as elect (established already, per the golden chain, by God's foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, and glorification) is itself the ground of their invulnerability to charge.

πείθω / πέπεισμαι — Active "To Persuade" vs. Perfect Passive "To Be Persuaded, Convinced"

See also the Perfect of Settled Confidence entry, section II.

Romans 8:38 — "For I am sure [πέπεισμαι]." The perfect passive form indicates a settled state of having been persuaded — a conviction produced in Paul by an outside agency (ultimately, doctrine perceived and believed), not a self-generated feeling of confidence.

ὑπερνικάω — "To Be More Than a Conqueror," Intensive Compound

A compound of ὑπέρ ("over, beyond") plus νικάω ("to conquer, to win"): to win a victory that exceeds or surpasses an ordinary victory.

Romans 8:37 — "we are more than conquerors [ὑπερνικῶμεν]" (see the Customary Present entry, section III) — the compound itself supplies the "more than" sense the English must render with two words: this is not mere survival or an ordinary win, but a victory that exceeds the category of "conquest" altogether — the specific reward of dying blessing given to the mature believer who wins through undeserved suffering.

χωρίζω — "To Separate," the Verb Governing the Closing Rhetorical Question

Romans 8:35, 39 — "Who shall separate [χωρίσει] us from the love of Christ?" and "nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate [χωρίσαι] us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" — the same verb frames both the opening question of the closing section and its final, comprehensive answer, forming an inclusio around the whole triumphant catalogue of Romans 8:35–39.

XX.Infinitives

Romans 1:15 — "so I am eager to preach the gospel to you also who are in Rome." εὐαγγελίσασθαι (aorist middle infinitive of εὐαγγελίζομαι) is an infinitive of intended result: the aorist gathers the totality of Paul's doctrinal communication into one entirety, while the infinitive marks the fulfillment of a deliberate objective.

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind." εἶναι (present active infinitive of εἰμί) functions within an accusative-plus-infinitive construction; its progressive present force denotes a persistent, continuing state — the legalist claims to be, and continues to be, a guide and a light.

Romans 2:21 — "you then who teach others, do you not teach yourself?" The articular present active infinitive of κηρύσσω ("to proclaim, to herald" — from κῆρυξ, the royal herald who delivered a king's proclamation to an assembled crowd) denotes the Judaizer's ongoing, one-directional public proclamation.

Infinitive of Actual Result

States a result that actually, in fact, occurred.

Romans 4:1 — "What then shall we say was gained by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh, has found?" εὑρηκέναι (the perfect infinitive of εὑρίσκω) presents Abraham's finding as an actual, historical result, not a merely conceivable or intended one (see also the Intensive Perfect entry, section II).

εἰς τό + Infinitive — Purpose Clauses

A fixed construction using the preposition εἰς with the articular infinitive, expressing purpose.

Romans 4:11 — "that he might be the father of all who believe without being circumcised, so that righteousness would be counted to them as well." εἰς τὸ εἶναι and the following purpose infinitive together express the design of God's arrangement with Abraham.

Romans 4:16 — "in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring." A comparable εἰς τό-type purpose construction underlies this clause.

Romans 4:18 — "In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations." εἰς τὸ γενέσθαι expresses the purpose/intended result of the promise given to Abraham.

Infinitive of Intended Result

States a result that is aimed at or intended, though not yet (or not necessarily) accomplished.

Romans 4:24 — "It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord." The infinitival construction underlying "who raised" (τὸν ἐγείραντα, more precisely a substantival participle functioning with infinitive-like purpose-force in the flow of the sentence) points to the intended result of resurrection faith extending forward to future believers.

Infinitive of Conceived Result

States a result that is merely conceived or imagined as a possibility, without asserting it as either intended or actual.

Romans 5:7 — "For one will scarcely die for a righteous person — though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die." The infinitive ἀποθανεῖν ("to die") here names a conceived, hypothetical result Paul is exploring for the sake of illustration, not an intended or actual result of any specific narrated event.

Articular Infinitive as a Purpose Clause

The infinitive with the definite article, functioning to express purpose.

Romans 6:12 — "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, to make you obey its passions." εἰς τὸ ὑπακούειν (articular infinitive with εἰς) expresses the purpose for which sin seeks to reign: specifically, to produce obedience to its own lusts.

Articular Infinitive as a Substantive Noun

The infinitive with the article functioning as the subject or object of a clause, essentially as a verbal noun.

Romans 7:18 — "For I know that nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh. For I have the desire to do what is right, but not the ability to carry it out." τὸ θέλειν ("the wishing/desiring," an articular infinitive) functions as a substantive, naming "the act of desiring" itself as a noun-like entity present in Paul — contrasted with τὸ κατεργάζεσθαι ("the accomplishing"), likewise substantival, which he finds absent.

Infinitive of Intended Resolve

States a settled resolve or determination — the will fixed on a course of action, whether or not that resolve is realized.

Romans 7:21 — "So I find it to be a law that when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand." τὸ ποιεῖν ("the doing," an articular infinitive) names Paul's fixed resolve to do what is honorable — the resolve itself, as a settled disposition, standing independent of whether it is successfully carried out (which, per the surrounding argument, it consistently is not, apart from the intervention described in Romans 8).

XXI.Selected Vocabulary in Doctrinal Context

τίς / οὖν / περισσός / Ἰουδαίου — The Opening Question of Romans 3

Romans 3:1 — "Then what advantage has the Jew? Or what is the value of circumcision?" τίς is the interrogative pronoun, nominative neuter, functioning as the subject of the clause. οὖν is the inferential particle drawing the question as a conclusion from chapter 2's demonstration of impartial justice. περισσός (rendered "advantage," more precisely "preeminence") is a predicate adjective used substantively, covering what exceeds the ordinary measure. Ἰουδαίου is a descriptive genitive covering both the racial and the national identity of Israel.

ὠφέλεια — Concrete Benefit or Profit

Romans 3:1 — "what is the value of circumcision?" ὠφέλεια (from ὠφελέω, "to help, to benefit") names concrete, accruing advantage or gain — distinct from the more abstract περισσός of the verse's first clause.

ἀσέβεια, ἀδικία — Recurring Across the Argument

See section XIX above for the full treatment at Romans 1:18; the same pairing recurs structurally across the letter as Paul's technical vocabulary for salvation maladjustment.

ἀνθρωποπάθεια — Anthropopathism as a Recurring Interpretive Key

Romans 1:18; 2:4; 9:13 — Divine anger, kindness, and hatred are each, in their turn, anthropopathisms: human characteristics ascribed to God as accommodations to human understanding, not literal descriptions of divine emotional experience. χρηστότης ("kindness"), ἀνοχή ("forbearance"), and μακροθυμία ("patience") at Romans 2:4 form a triad of such anthropopathisms describing the comprehensive fullness of what God's justice has arranged for the sinner who will adjust to it.

μετάνοια — Change of Mind, Not Emotional Remorse

Romans 2:4 — "not knowing that God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance." μετάνοια denotes a change of mind — specifically, in the evangelistic sense, the change of mind toward Christ that constitutes positive volition at gospel hearing, not sorrow or emotional contrition as an end in itself.

σκληρότης — The Scar Tissue of the Soul

Romans 2:5 — "But because of your hard and impenitent heart." σκληρότης ("hardness") names the scarring that accumulates in the soul through sustained negative volition — a doctrinal category, not merely a description of stubbornness.

καρδία — The Right Lobe of the Soul

Romans 2:5, 29 — "your hard and impenitent heart"; "circumcision is a matter of the heart." καρδία in Paul's technical usage designates the right lobe of the soul — the seat of doctrine storage, thinking, and the capacity for faith — not the physical organ nor a purely emotional center.

δόξα, τιμή, ἀφθαρσία — Three Results of Salvation Adjustment

Romans 2:7 — "to those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality." δόξα ("glory"), τιμή ("honor," something of intrinsic worth, tracing from Homeric material wealth to Attic abstract integrity), and ἀφθαρσία ("incorruptibility," the technical term for the future resurrection body) together name the guaranteed, irrevocable results of salvation adjustment, established at the moment of faith and resting on the integrity of the God who provides them.

θλῖψις, στενοχωρία — Personal and National Distress

Romans 2:9 — "There will be tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil." θλῖψις names pressure at the personal level; στενοχωρία (στενός, "narrow" + χώρα, "space") names a constricted environment — the national and historical level of temporal judgment, the collapse of a civilization's room to maneuver under the cycles of divine discipline.

Ἕλλην vs. ἔθνος — Culture Versus Bare Ethnicity

Romans 2:9–10 — Paul's choice of Ἕλλην ("Greek," a person shaped by Hellenistic culture — philosophy, rhetoric, art) rather than the more generic ἔθνος ("nation, Gentile") establishes the cultural dimension of the impartiality principle: no level of cultural achievement, any more than racial privilege, constitutes standing before the justice of God.

ὁδηγός τυφλῶν, φῶς — Ironic Self-Description

Romans 2:19 — "and if you are sure that you yourself are a guide to the blind, a light to those who are in darkness." ὁδηγός ("guide," from ὁδός + ἡγέομαι) and φῶς ("light," echoing Isaiah 49:6's description of the Servant of Jehovah) are both deployed ironically: the self-righteous legalist claims titles that belong properly to Christ and to a nation adjusted to God's justice, while himself remaining blind.

δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ — The Recurring Central Phrase

Romans 1:17; 3:5, 21, 22 — δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ is the organizing phrase of the entire epistle: God's perfect righteousness and justice in unified operation. The anarthrous construction at 3:5 emphasizes the quality of God's integrity rather than merely identifying it, and — the source material notes — also signals the debater's own ignorance of what that integrity actually is.

δικαιόω — Vindication, Not Only Justification

Romans 3:4 — "Let God be true though every one were a liar... that you may be justified in your words." δικαιόω, ranging in meaning from "to regard as right" to "to acquit" to "to vindicate," here refers to the vindication of divine integrity through the doctrines of Scripture, distinct from its more familiar soteriological sense of the judicial declaration of righteousness at salvation (as at 3:24, 26).

διαστολή — No Distinction

Romans 3:22 — "for there is no distinction." διαστολή states that Jew and Gentile alike stand in identical condemnation under spiritual death, with no inherent advantage before the justice of God — the structural hinge of the entire argument from 1:18 through 3:20.

ἁμαρτάνω — To Sin, to Miss the Mark

Romans 3:23 — "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." ἁμαρτάνω, in the constative/gnomic aorist ἥμαρτον (see Aorist Tense entries), gathers all sin of the entire human race into one axiomatic statement, stated as a fact so fixed as to require no further argument.

λογίζομαι — Rigorous Inference, Not Casual Reflection

Romans 3:28 — "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." λογίζομαι, present middle indicative, denotes in classical usage an act of thought according to strict logical rules — here, "we conclude then," marking a self-evident logical outcome from the doctrine of divine integrity combined with propitiation.

νόμος πίστεως vs. νόμος ἔργων — Two Contrasted Principles

Romans 3:27 — νόμος πίστεως ("the law/principle of faith") is the fixed, invariable divine principle that a right relationship with God's integrity is received through non-meritorious faith in Christ alone, apart from any human work — contrasted directly with νόμος ἔργων ("the law/principle of works").

ἔθνος (genitive plural) — God of the Gentiles Too

Romans 3:29 — "Or is God the God of Jews only? Is he not the God of Gentiles also?" The objective genitive plural ἐθνῶν affirms that God is the God of the Gentile nations as fully as of Israel — the same integrity governs both.

λογίζομαι — "To Reckon, To Credit, To Consider"

The technical accounting/legal term underlying the entire doctrine of imputation across Romans 4–6.

Romans 4:3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23, 24 — the verb recurs throughout the Abraham argument in its technical sense of crediting something to someone's account, the central vocabulary item of the imputation doctrine developed across the whole chapter.

Romans 6:11 — "consider [λογίζεσθε] yourselves dead to sin and alive to God" — the same verb, shifted from a purely judicial/forensic sense (imputation of righteousness) to a volitional, applicatory sense (the believer's own reckoning of a settled fact about himself).

στοιχέω — "To Walk in Line With, To Walk by a Rule or Standard"

Originally a military term for walking in formation, rank by rank; used metaphorically of consistent conduct according to a fixed standard.

Romans 4:12 — "who also walk in the footsteps of the faith that our father Abraham had" — the underlying verb behind "walk" here carries the sense of consistent, disciplined conformity to a pattern, not casual or occasional resemblance (see the Perfective Present entry, section III).

δικαιόω + τὸν ἀσεβῆ — "Justifies the Ungodly," a Doctrinally Startling Combination

The verb δικαιόω ("to justify, to declare righteous") combined with the object τὸν ἀσεβῆ ("the ungodly one") forms one of the most doctrinally loaded phrases in the chapter, since ordinary legal usage would never permit "justifying" (declaring righteous) one who is, in fact, guilty.

Romans 4:5 — "believes in him who justifies the ungodly" — the phrase is deliberately startling by the standard of ordinary judicial language: God's justification is not a declaration that the ungodly person is, in fact, innocent (which would be a legal fiction) but an act founded on imputed righteousness received by faith — a distinct category from ordinary human courtroom justification.

σφραγίς — "Seal," a Legal/Authenticating Mark

A term denoting an official seal, used to authenticate a document or certify ownership/completion.

Romans 4:11 — "he received the sign of circumcision as a seal [σφραγῖδα] of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised." Circumcision functions here as a legal-authenticating mark certifying a righteousness Abraham already possessed by faith — the seal does not produce the righteousness, it certifies a prior, already-existing fact.

ζωοποιέω — "To Make Alive, To Give Life"

A compound of ζωή ("life") plus ποιέω ("to make, to do").

Romans 4:17 — "who gives life [ζωοποιοῦντος] to the dead" — describing God's characteristic action in Abraham and Sarah's case (restoring reproductive capacity to bodies as good as dead) and, by extension, his characteristic power generally.

Romans 8:11 — "he... will also give life [ζωοποιήσει] to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" — the same verb, now applied to the future bodily resurrection of the believer.

νέκρωσις — "Deadness," the State/Condition of Being as Good as Dead

Romans 4:19 — the underlying description of Sarah's womb and Abraham's own body draws on this term-family: the condition in view is total, comprehensive incapacity, not partial weakness.

ἐνδυναμόω — "To Empower, To Strengthen"

A compound of ἐν ("in") plus δύναμις ("power"): to infuse with power from within.

Romans 4:20 — "he grew strong [ἐνεδυναμώθη] in his faith as he gave glory to God" (see the Combinative Aorist entry, section I) — the verb specifically denotes power supplied from an internal, resident source (doctrine converted to capacity), not merely an increase in resolve.

πληροφορέω — "To Fully Persuade, To Bring to Full Conviction"

A compound of πλήρης ("full") plus φορέω (an intensive form of φέρω, "to carry, to bear"): to be fully carried along to a settled conviction.

Romans 4:21 — "fully convinced [πληροφορηθείς] that God was able to do what he had promised" — see also the discussion of this term's distinction from πληρόω under section XIX.

εἰρήνη — "Peace," Technical Sense of Prosperity/Well-Being

Beyond the ordinary sense of absence of conflict, εἰρήνη in Paul frequently carries the fuller Hebraic sense of shalom: total well-being, prosperity, right relationship.

Romans 5:1 — "we have [or, let us have] peace with God" — the term names the restored, prosperous relational condition between God and the believer established at justification, not merely a cessation of hostility.

προσαγωγή — "Access, Introduction"

A term denoting formal introduction into the presence of a superior — court-language, describing being formally ushered into royal presence.

Romans 5:2 — "through whom we have also obtained access [προσαγωγήν] by faith into this grace in which we stand." The term's court-introduction background reinforces the formal, granted (not self-achieved) nature of the believer's standing before God.

θλῖψις — "Tribulation, Pressure, Affliction"

Literally denotes pressure or crushing, often used of external circumstantial pressure or persecution.

Romans 5:3 — "we rejoice in our sufferings [θλίψεσιν]" — external, circumstantial pressure specifically, the category of undeserved suffering under discussion throughout this stretch of the argument.

ὑπομονή — "Endurance, Steadfastness Under Pressure"

A compound of ὑπό ("under") plus μονή (from μένω, "to remain, to abide"): literally, "remaining under" — the capacity to stay in place under sustained pressure rather than collapsing or fleeing.

Romans 5:3–4 — "suffering produces endurance [ὑπομονήν]" — the term names the specific capacity produced by properly processed θλῖψις: not mere passive tolerance, but active, sustained remaining-under.

δοκιμή — "Proven Character, Tested Approval"

A term denoting the quality that results from having been tested and having passed the test — approved character.

Romans 5:4 — "and endurance produces character [δοκιμήν]" — the tested, approved quality of soul that results when ὑπομονή is sustained to its completion.

ἐλπίς — "Hope," Technical Sense of Confident Expectation

Not a wishful uncertainty (the ordinary English sense of "hope") but a confident, doctrinally-grounded expectation of a certain future.

Romans 5:4–5 — "and character produces hope [ἐλπίδα], and hope does not put us to shame" — the term names the confident forward-orientation that results from the completed suffering-endurance-character sequence, and which cannot disappoint precisely because it rests on the settled character of God, not on uncertain circumstances.

ἀσθενής — "Weak, Without Strength"

Romans 5:6 — "while we were still weak [ἀσθενεῖς]" — describing the total incapacity of the human race, apart from Christ, to remedy its own condition.

ἀσεβής — "Ungodly, Impious"

Romans 5:6 — "Christ died for the ungodly [ἀσεβῶν]" — the same term discussed at Romans 4:5 above, here identifying the object of Christ's substitutionary death: not the righteous, but those in active opposition to or neglect of God.

ἁμαρτωλός — "Sinner," One Characterized by Sin

Romans 5:8 — "while we were still sinners [ἁμαρτωλοί], Christ died for us" — the noun form, naming the human race by its defining characteristic at the time Christ's death occurred.

ἐχθρός — "Enemy"

Romans 5:10 — "For if while we were enemies [ἐχθροί] we were reconciled to God" — describing the state of active hostility between the unregenerate human race and God prior to reconciliation.

καταλλάσσω — "To Reconcile," Technical Term for Restoring Broken Relationship

Romans 5:10 (twice) — "we were reconciled [κατηλλάγημεν] to God by the death of his Son... having been reconciled [καταλλαγέντες], shall we be saved" — the verb names the specific act of removing the barrier of enmity and restoring right relationship, distinct from justification (which addresses the legal verdict) though accomplished by the same saving work.

αἷμα — "Blood," Metonym for Sacrificial Death

Romans 5:9 — "we have now been justified by his blood [αἵματι]" — "blood" functions here as a metonym for the entirety of Christ's sacrificial, substitutionary death, not a reference to the physical liquid in isolation.

τύπος — "Type, Pattern, Prefigurement"

A term denoting a prefiguring pattern that anticipates and corresponds to a later, greater reality.

Romans 5:14 — "who was a type [τύπος] of the one who was to come" — Adam functions as a τύπος of Christ: a historical pattern whose very structure (one man's act determining the destiny of the many he represents) anticipates and corresponds to the greater reality accomplished in Christ.

ὁμοίωμα — "Likeness" (see also section XIX's fuller entry at Romans 8:3)

Romans 5:14 — "even over those whose sinning was not like [in the] likeness [ὁμοιώματι] of the transgression of Adam" — the term here distinguishes personal sins (committed between Adam and Moses) from Adam's specific, unique transgression against a direct, explicit command — the death that reigned over that intervening period did not depend on personal sins bearing an identical structure to Adam's original violation.

βαπτίζω — "To Baptize," Technical Term for the Spirit's Placing Work

Romans 6:3 (twice) — "all of us who have been baptized [ἐβαπτίσθημεν] into Christ Jesus were baptized [ἐβαπτίσθημεν] into his death" — the term here refers exclusively to the baptism of the Holy Spirit (the placing of the believer into union with Christ at the moment of salvation), not to water baptism, which functions elsewhere in the New Testament purely as a public picture/testimony of this prior spiritual reality.

ἀγνοέω vs. γινώσκω — "To Be Ignorant Of" vs. "To Know"

Simple antonyms, but Paul's deliberate choice between them structures his rhetorical questions.

Romans 6:3 — "Do you not know [ἀγνοεῖτε, "are you ignorant"]...?" — the negative form of the question assumes the Roman believers ought already to possess this knowledge, making their hypothetical ignorance rhetorically pointed.

Romans 7:1 — "Do you not know, brothers... that the law is binding on a person only as long as he lives?" — same rhetorical pattern, same verb, opening the marriage-analogy argument of chapter 7.

παρίστημι — "To Present, To Place At Someone's Disposal"

A term with a range of technical uses: presenting a sacrifice, presenting oneself for service, placing something or someone at another's disposal for use.

Romans 6:13, 16, 19 (see the Constative Aorist and Pictorial Present entries, sections I and III) — the verb recurs as the central vocabulary item of the "presenting" imagery running through the second half of chapter 6: the believer is repeatedly commanded to place himself, and his bodily members, at the disposal of either the sin nature or God, as an act of daily volitional surrender rather than a one-time gesture.

ἅγιος / δίκαιος / ἀγαθός — The Triad Describing the Law (and, by Extension, God's Own Character)

Three adjectives applied together to the Mosaic law, each naming a distinct facet of its perfection.

Romans 7:12 — "So the law is holy [ἅγιος], and the commandment is holy [ἁγία] and righteous [δικαία] and good [ἀγαθή]." ἅγιος names the law's set-apart, consecrated character; δίκαιος names its perfect conformity to justice; ἀγαθός names its beneficial, good intent and effect — together, a comprehensive threefold defense of the law's own character against any suggestion (explicitly rejected at v.7 and v.13 by μὴ γένοιτο) that the law itself is somehow implicated in producing sin or death.

καρπός — "Fruit," Metaphor for the Result of a Governing Relationship

Romans 6:21–22 and Romans 7:4–5 both use καρπός/καρποφορέω metaphorically: the "fruit" produced by a life is determined by which husband/master governs it — shame and death as the fruit of the old marriage to the sin nature, sanctification and the fruit borne "for God" as the fruit of the new marriage established through Christ's body.

νόμος — Three Distinguishable Usages, Especially Concentrated in Romans 8:2

The single noun νόμος ("law") carries at least three distinguishable senses across the passage as a whole, and Paul deliberately exploits the ambiguity within a single verse at Romans 8:2.

The Mosaic Law proper, the historical body of legislation given through Moses — the dominant sense throughout Romans 7 (e.g., 7:1, 7, 12, 14).

A governing "principle" or "law" in the sense of a fixed, operative rule or force, not a body of legislation at all — this sense governs both occurrences of νόμος within Romans 8:2 itself: "the law [principle] of the Spirit of life" and "the law [principle] of sin and death."

A "law" discovered by observation, describing a recurring pattern of experience — Romans 7:21, "So I find it to be a law that when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand," uses νόμος in this observational, almost scientific sense: a regularity Paul has discovered empirically in his own experience, not a reference to the Mosaic legislation at all.

Gaps in Coverage — Stated Plainly

Romans 4:15 has no corresponding source chapter in the manuscript material processed to build this guide. No entries exist for this verse, and none have been fabricated to fill the gap. If source material for 4:15 becomes available, it can be added at that time.

Second-class conditions (contrary-to-fact) and fourth-class conditions (remote possibility) are named and defined in the source manuscript's own four-class framework (the framework is explicitly laid out in connection with Romans 7:2), but no verse processed here is actually labeled by the source as an instance of either class. This is a gap in what the source material happens to illustrate, not a defect in the underlying grammatical categories, which are real and well-attested elsewhere in Greek. The one illustration of a second-class-condition shape offered above (1 Corinthians 15:17) is included only to show what the category looks like in the abstract; it is explicitly not a Romans example and was not treated as one. Likewise, an example proposed earlier in this project's discussion ("if Christ was not raised, our faith is void") was not incorporated anywhere in this guide, since it is not Romans material.

A passage on adoption and the Abba-Father cry, initially found positioned within the Romans 6 portion of the source manuscript, was checked against the actual Romans 8 material and confirmed not to be a duplicate: it supplies unique content (the Roman legal/ceremonial background of adoption, and the κράζω/Abba-Father bilingual-cry analysis) not otherwise present in the Romans 8 source file. That content has been folded into the υἱοθεσία and κράζω entries in section XIX above, where it belongs by subject matter, rather than left stranded under Romans 6.

Romans 1–3 were added from three further manuscript files (covering 1:1–32, 2:1–29, and 3:1–31 in full — the Romans 3 material was initially thought to stop at verse 3, but on full reading proved to cover the entire chapter through verse 31, spread across roughly thirty manuscript chapters that were not all individually marked with their own heading in the source document). Coverage here was built by full-text extraction across each manuscript's own glossary and definitional material — every citation traces to a real, explicit statement in the source about that specific Greek form in that specific verse — rather than a page-by-page narrative read of the full manuscript prose. As with Romans 4–8, no citation is fabricated or inferred beyond what the source states.

This concludes the guide as built from the source material processed to date (Romans 1:1–32, 2:1–29, 3:1–31, 4:1–25, 5:1–21, 6:1–23, 7:1–25, 8:1–39). No citation anywhere in this document is compressed to a bare verse list or shortcut phrase — every citation carries its own full quotation, Greek term, and explanation.
